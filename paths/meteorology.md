# Meteorology

The science of weather: how the atmosphere works, clouds, winds and pressure, fronts, storms, cyclones and monsoons, how forecasts are made from satellites to supercomputers and AI, and extremes, climate and warnings that save lives. About 150 to 180 hours over six to eight months.

**Access and balance.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. Free courses, national weather services, the WMO, live weather maps and forecast centres cover the core. You need school physics and geography. Look up at the sky and check forecasts every day: weather is the one science you can study from any window.

# About meteorology

Meteorology studies the atmosphere and its weather: temperature, pressure, wind, clouds, rain and storms, and how to forecast them. It underpins farming, aviation, shipping, energy and public safety.

This path begins with how forecasts reach you and how to read the sky, explains the atmosphere's structure and motions, studies fronts, thunderstorms, tropical cyclones and monsoons, follows forecasting from a hand-calculated dream to supercomputers and AI, examines extremes, climate links and early warnings, and ends with citizen science and research.

**Trivia**
- Luke Howard gave clouds their Latin names, such as cumulus, stratus and cirrus, in 1802.
- Admiral Robert FitzRoy coined the word “forecast” for weather predictions in the 1860s.
- Lewis Fry Richardson imagined a “forecast factory” of 64,000 people calculating the weather by hand.
- Weather forecasts have improved by about a day of accuracy per decade: a five-day forecast today is as good as a much shorter one decades ago.

### Timeline
- **1643 · Evangelista Torricelli** invents the barometer.
- **1735 · George Hadley** explains the trade winds.
- **1802 · Luke Howard** names the clouds.
- **1861 · Robert FitzRoy** publishes the first public weather forecasts in Britain.
- **1919 · The Bergen school** describes weather fronts.
- **1922 · Lewis Fry Richardson** attempts a numerical weather forecast by hand.
- **1950 · The ENIAC computer** makes the first computer weather forecast.
- **1960 · TIROS-1** sends the first weather satellite images.
- **1963 · Edward Lorenz** shows weather is chaotic.
- **2023 · AI weather models** match traditional forecasts on many measures.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will understand weather the way a strong introductory meteorology course expects. You will be able to:

- **Explain the atmosphere:** layers, pressure, humidity, stability and global circulation.
- **Explain weather systems:** fronts, thunderstorms, tropical cyclones and monsoons.
- **Read forecasts and maps:** including ensembles and probabilities.
- **Explain how forecasts are made:** observations, satellites, numerical models and AI.
- **Connect weather and climate:** extremes, attribution and early warning systems.
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check.

These goals are a draft standard. Meteorologists and atmospheric science educators from more than one country will confirm them before the path is published.

**How stages work.** Each work ends with a short quiz. Score 70% or more across a stage's quizzes to pass it. If you fall short, you can retake the quizzes, or continue anyway: the stage then stays marked **Not finished** until you come back and pass it.
