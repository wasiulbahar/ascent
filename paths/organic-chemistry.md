# Organic Chemistry

The chemistry of carbon: structure and bonding, naming and stereochemistry, reaction mechanisms, spectroscopy and lab technique, synthesis, and organic chemistry in medicines, materials and green chemistry. About 250 to 300 hours over ten to twelve months.

**Access and balance.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. Free open textbooks and university lectures cover the core. You need school chemistry; the Chemistry path is a good start. Only do practical work in a supervised laboratory with proper safety equipment.

# About organic chemistry

Organic chemistry studies compounds of carbon, which can form an enormous variety of chains, rings and shapes. It is the chemistry of life, medicines, plastics, dyes, fuels and food.

This path starts with the history and the big picture, teaches structure, naming and 3D shape, explains how reactions happen with curved arrows, shows how chemists identify molecules and work in the lab, builds to planning syntheses, and ends with drug discovery, materials and green chemistry.

**Trivia**
- In 1828 Friedrich Wöhler made urea from inorganic materials, undermining the idea that organic compounds needed a “vital force”.
- William Perkin discovered the first synthetic dye, mauve, in 1856 when he was 18.
- August Kekulé later said the ring structure of benzene came to him in a daydream of a snake biting its tail.
- Thalidomide's tragedy in the late 1950s and 1960s showed that mirror-image molecules can act very differently, and led to stricter drug testing.

### Timeline
- **1828 · Friedrich Wöhler** synthesises urea.
- **1856 · William Perkin** makes mauve, the first synthetic dye.
- **1858 · Kekulé and Couper** propose that carbon forms four bonds.
- **1865 · August Kekulé** proposes the ring structure of benzene.
- **1874 · Van 't Hoff and Le Bel** propose the tetrahedral carbon atom.
- **1897 · Felix Hoffmann at Bayer** makes a pure, stable aspirin.
- **1965 · Robert Burns Woodward** wins the Nobel Prize for organic synthesis.
- **1990 · E. J. Corey** wins the Nobel Prize for retrosynthetic analysis.
- **2010 · Heck, Negishi and Suzuki** win the Nobel Prize for cross-coupling.
- **2022 · Bertozzi, Meldal and Sharpless** win the Nobel Prize for click chemistry.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will know organic chemistry the way a strong two-semester university course expects. You will be able to:

- **Describe structure:** bonding, functional groups, names and three-dimensional shape.
- **Explain mechanisms:** with curved arrows, for substitution, elimination, addition and carbonyl reactions.
- **Identify molecules:** from NMR, IR and mass spectra.
- **Plan syntheses:** working backwards from a target with retrosynthesis.
- **Connect to the world:** medicines, polymers and greener chemistry.
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check.

These goals are a draft standard. Chemists and chemistry educators from more than one country will confirm them before the path is published.

**How stages work.** Each work ends with a short quiz. Score 70% or more across a stage's quizzes to pass it. If you fall short, you can retake the quizzes, or continue anyway: the stage then stays marked **Not finished** until you come back and pass it.

# Final mastery check

Finishing all six stages makes you a capable organic chemist on paper. To earn the **Ascent Mastery: Organic Chemistry** certificate, you also complete:

1. **The grand quiz.** 40 game-style questions across all six stages. Pass mark 80%, with unlimited retakes.
2. **Mechanism set.** Draw mechanisms for unseen reactions.
3. **Spectra puzzle.** Identify unknown compounds from spectra.
4. **Capstone project.** Plan a full synthesis of a real target molecule and justify each step in 2,500 words. Strong projects are read by an expert reviewer.
5. **Explain it.** Answer three unseen questions as if to a friend. For example: “Why does a mirror-image drug act differently?”

The certificate needs every stage passed. It does not qualify you for unsupervised lab work.
