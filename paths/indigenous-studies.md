# Indigenous Studies

The histories, knowledges, cultures and rights of the world's Indigenous peoples: from the Americas, the Pacific, Australia, the Arctic, Africa and Asia; colonisation and resistance; land, language and law; Indigenous knowledge and science; and self-determination today. About 250 to 300 hours over ten to twelve months.

**Access and balance.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. Many Indigenous organisations, archives and UN bodies share material free. The path centres Indigenous authors and voices, covers peoples on every inhabited continent, respects that some knowledge is not meant to be shared publicly, and presents debates fairly.

# About Indigenous studies

There are over 476 million Indigenous people in more than 90 countries, belonging to about 5,000 distinct cultures and speaking most of the world's languages. Indigenous studies centres their histories, knowledges and perspectives, and examines colonisation, resistance and self-determination.

This path begins with Indigenous writers and storytellers, studies diverse Indigenous societies, examines colonisation and its legacies, explores land, language and law, looks at Indigenous knowledge, science and art, and ends with self-determination, rights and research ethics.

**Trivia**
- About 476 million Indigenous people live in more than 90 countries, around 6% of the world's population.
- Of the world's roughly 7,000 languages, a large share are Indigenous, and many are endangered.
- The UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples was adopted in 2007 after over 20 years of negotiation.
- In 1992 Australia's High Court overturned the doctrine of terra nullius in the Mabo case.

### Timeline
- **1493 · The papal bull *Inter caetera*** underpins the “Doctrine of Discovery”.
- **1840 · The Treaty of Waitangi** is signed between Māori and the British Crown.
- **1876 · Canada's Indian Act** is passed.
- **1960s–70s · Indigenous rights movements** grow worldwide.
- **1975 · The Waitangi Tribunal** is set up in New Zealand.
- **1982 · The UN Working Group on Indigenous Populations** begins.
- **1992 · The Mabo decision** overturns terra nullius in Australia.
- **2007 · UNDRIP** is adopted by the UN General Assembly.
- **2015 · Canada's Truth and Reconciliation Commission** issues its final report.
- **2022–2032 · The International Decade of Indigenous Languages** is declared by the UN.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will know Indigenous studies the way a strong first year of a university course expects. You will be able to:

- **Describe Indigenous diversity:** peoples, societies and knowledges across the world.
- **Explain colonisation and its legacies:** dispossession, residential schools and resistance.
- **Explain land, language and law:** treaties, title, language revitalisation and rights.
- **Engage with Indigenous knowledge:** respectfully, including its relationship with science.
- **Discuss self-determination and research ethics:** UNDRIP, sovereignty and Indigenous-led research.
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check.

These goals are a draft standard. Indigenous scholars and communities from several regions will review the path before it is published.

**How stages work.** Each work ends with a short quiz. Score 70% or more across a stage's quizzes to pass it. If you fall short, you can retake the quizzes, or continue anyway: the stage then stays marked **Not finished** until you come back and pass it.

# Final mastery check

Finishing all six stages makes you a capable student of Indigenous studies. To earn the **Ascent Mastery: Indigenous Studies** certificate, you also complete:

1. **The grand quiz.** 40 game-style questions across all six stages. Pass mark 80%, with unlimited retakes.
2. **Case study.** Research one Indigenous nation's history and current struggles using its own sources.
3. **Rights analysis.** Apply UNDRIP to an unseen case.
4. **Capstone essay.** 2,500 words on one of the Stage 6 questions. Strong essays are read by an expert reviewer.
5. **Explain it.** Answer three unseen questions as if to a curious friend. For example: “Who are Indigenous peoples?”

The certificate needs every stage passed.
