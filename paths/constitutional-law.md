# Constitutional Law

How constitutions create, divide and limit power: separation of powers, judicial review, rights and their limits, federalism, emergencies and democratic backsliding, compared across many countries. About 150 to 200 hours over six to eight months.

**Access and balance.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. Constitutions, landmark judgments and classic texts are free online. No legal training is needed. Cases come from many countries so you learn to compare, not memorise one system.

# About constitutional law

Constitutional law is the law about the law: how a state is organised, who holds power, how that power is checked, and which rights people hold against it.

This path starts with what a constitution is, reads the classic theory of divided power, studies courts and judicial review, explores rights and proportionality across countries, examines federalism, emergencies and democratic decline, and ends with constitution-making and current research.

**Trivia**
- The United Kingdom has no single written constitution; its rules are spread across statutes, cases and conventions.
- India's constitution is among the longest in the world, with hundreds of articles.
- Germany's Basic Law of 1949 opens with human dignity: “Human dignity shall be inviolable.”
- South Africa's Constitutional Court abolished the death penalty in its first major case, in 1995.

### Timeline
- **1215 · Magna Carta** limits the power of the English king.
- **1689 · John Locke** argues government rests on consent.
- **1748 · Montesquieu** describes the separation of powers.
- **1787 · The United States Constitution** is drafted in Philadelphia.
- **1803 · *Marbury v. Madison*** establishes judicial review in the United States.
- **1920 · Austria** creates a specialised constitutional court, designed with Hans Kelsen.
- **1949 · Germany's Basic Law** puts human dignity first.
- **1973 · India's Supreme Court** announces the basic structure doctrine.
- **1982 · Canada** adopts the Charter of Rights and Freedoms.
- **1996 · South Africa** adopts its post-apartheid constitution.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will know constitutional law the way a strong undergraduate course expects. You will be able to:

- **Explain what constitutions do:** create, divide and limit power.
- **Explain judicial review:** its origins, forms and the debate over it.
- **Analyse rights cases:** using proportionality and comparison.
- **Explain federalism and emergencies:** and how democracies erode.
- **Compare systems:** across several countries and legal traditions.
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check.

These goals are a draft standard. Constitutional scholars from more than one country will confirm them before the path is published.

**How stages work.** Each work ends with a short quiz. Score 70% or more across a stage's quizzes to pass it. If you fall short, you can retake the quizzes, or continue anyway: the stage then stays marked **Not finished** until you come back and pass it.
