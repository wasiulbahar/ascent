# Forensic Science

How science serves justice: the principles of trace evidence, DNA, fingerprints, toxicology and bones, crime scenes, labs and digital evidence, error, bias and wrongful convictions, forensics for human rights and disasters, and the future of evidence in court. About 120 to 150 hours over five to six months.

**Access and balance.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. Free reports from standards bodies, the UN, the WHO and innocence organisations cover the core. You need school science. The path is honest about forensic science's limits as well as its strengths, and covers its use for human rights worldwide. Real forensic work requires professional training.

# About forensic science

Forensic science applies scientific methods to questions of law: identifying people, linking evidence to events, and determining causes of death, from courtrooms to mass graves and disaster sites.

This path begins with the founding principle that every contact leaves a trace, studies DNA, fingerprints, toxicology and bones, covers crime scenes, chain of custody and digital evidence, faces the evidence on error and bias, explores human rights and disaster identification, and ends with genetic genealogy, statistics and expert testimony.

**Trivia**
- Edmond Locard, who opened a police laboratory in Lyon in 1910, taught that “every contact leaves a trace”.
- DNA profiling's first criminal case, in England in the 1980s, first cleared an innocent suspect before catching the killer.
- In 2004 the FBI wrongly matched an American lawyer's fingerprint to the Madrid train bombings, prompting reviews of fingerprint evidence.
- Argentina's Forensic Anthropology Team, founded in 1984, has helped identify victims of political violence in dozens of countries.

### Timeline
- **1813 · Mathieu Orfila** publishes a founding treatise on toxicology.
- **1892 · Francis Galton** publishes *Finger Prints*.
- **1901 · Scotland Yard** adopts the Henry fingerprint system.
- **1910 · Edmond Locard** opens a forensic laboratory in Lyon.
- **1984 · Alec Jeffreys** discovers DNA fingerprinting; the Argentine Forensic Anthropology Team is founded.
- **1988 · Colin Pitchfork** becomes the first person convicted using DNA evidence.
- **1992 · The Innocence Project** is founded.
- **2009 · The US National Academy of Sciences** criticises the scientific basis of many forensic methods.
- **2016 · The Minnesota Protocol** sets standards for investigating unlawful deaths.
- **2018 · Genetic genealogy** identifies the Golden State Killer.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will understand forensic science the way a strong introductory course expects. You will be able to:

- **Explain trace evidence:** Locard's principle and how evidence is collected and preserved.
- **Explain key disciplines:** DNA, fingerprints, toxicology, anthropology and digital forensics.
- **Judge reliability:** error rates, cognitive bias and lessons from wrongful convictions.
- **Explain forensics for justice:** human rights investigations and disaster victim identification.
- **Weigh evidence in court:** likelihood ratios, expert testimony and new technologies.
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check.

These goals are a draft standard. Forensic scientists, lawyers and human rights investigators from more than one country will confirm them before the path is published.

**How stages work.** Each work ends with a short quiz. Score 70% or more across a stage's quizzes to pass it. If you fall short, you can retake the quizzes, or continue anyway: the stage then stays marked **Not finished** until you come back and pass it.

# Final mastery check

Finishing all six stages gives you a strong understanding of forensic science. To earn the **Ascent Mastery: Forensic Science** certificate, you also complete:

1. **The grand quiz.** 40 game-style questions across all six stages. Pass mark 80%, with unlimited retakes.
2. **Case review.** Analyse a real case where forensic evidence was crucial or flawed.
3. **Evidence plan.** Plan how evidence should be collected, preserved and analysed in a fictional scenario.
4. **Capstone essay.** 2,500 words on one of the Stage 6 questions. Strong essays are read by an expert reviewer.
5. **Explain it.** Answer three unseen questions as if to a juror. For example: “How sure is a DNA match?”

The certificate needs every stage passed. It is not a professional forensic qualification.
