# Data Visualisation

How to turn data into clear, honest pictures: perception and design principles, colour, tools from spreadsheets to code, maps and the history of great charts, uncertainty, accessibility and honesty, and building a portfolio. About 120 to 150 hours over five to six months.

**Access and balance.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. Free books, tools, galleries and weekly practice datasets cover the core. You need basic spreadsheet skills; coding is optional but taught. Make a chart at every stage: visualisation is learned by doing and redoing.

# About data visualisation

Data visualisation is the craft of showing data as graphics so people can see patterns, compare values and understand uncertainty. Good charts inform decisions; bad ones mislead.

This path begins with inspiring examples and interactive tools, teaches how people perceive graphics and how to use colour, builds skills in code and no-code tools, studies maps and the history of great charts, covers uncertainty, accessibility and honesty, and ends with practice, data journalism and research.

**Trivia**
- William Playfair invented the line and bar chart in 1786 and the pie chart in 1801.
- Charles Minard's 1869 chart of Napoleon's Russian campaign shows six kinds of data in one image.
- Florence Nightingale became the first woman elected to the Royal Statistical Society in 1858, partly for her diagrams of army deaths.
- For the 1900 Paris Exposition, W. E. B. Du Bois and his students hand-drew striking charts of Black life in the United States.

### Timeline
- **1786 · William Playfair** publishes the first line and bar charts.
- **1854 · John Snow** maps cholera deaths in London.
- **1858 · Florence Nightingale** publishes her rose diagrams.
- **1869 · Charles Minard** charts Napoleon's march on Moscow.
- **1900 · W. E. B. Du Bois** shows data portraits at the Paris Exposition.
- **1977 · John Tukey** publishes *Exploratory Data Analysis*.
- **1983 · Edward Tufte** publishes *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*.
- **1984 · Cleveland and McGill** rank how accurately people read graphical forms.
- **2006 · Hans Rosling** popularises animated bubble charts.
- **2011 · D3.js** is released for interactive web graphics.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will make charts the way a skilled data journalist or analyst does. You will be able to:

- **Choose forms:** match chart types to questions, using what we know about perception.
- **Design clearly:** colour, labels, annotation and layout that guide the eye.
- **Build charts:** in a no-code tool and in R, Python or JavaScript.
- **Show uncertainty and maps:** honestly, including the tricks maps can play.
- **Make charts accessible and honest:** for every reader, without misleading.
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check.

These goals are a draft standard. Data journalists, designers and statisticians from more than one country will confirm them before the path is published.

**How stages work.** Each work ends with a short quiz. Score 70% or more across a stage's quizzes to pass it. If you fall short, you can retake the quizzes, or continue anyway: the stage then stays marked **Not finished** until you come back and pass it.

# Final mastery check

Finishing all six stages makes you a skilled maker of charts. To earn the **Ascent Mastery: Data Visualisation** certificate, you also complete:

1. **The grand quiz.** 40 game-style questions across all six stages. Pass mark 80%, with unlimited retakes.
2. **Portfolio.** Six original charts or maps, each with a short design rationale.
3. **Redesign.** Redesign a misleading or confusing published chart and explain every change.
4. **Capstone story.** A data story of 1,500 words with charts. Strong stories are read by an expert reviewer.
5. **Explain it.** Answer three unseen questions as if to a colleague. For example: “Why shouldn't I use a 3D pie chart?”

The certificate needs every stage passed.
