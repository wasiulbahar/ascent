# Game Theory

The mathematics of strategic choices: Nash equilibrium and mixed strategies, sequential and repeated games, games with hidden information, auctions, matching and mechanism design, evolution and cooperation, and applications from nuclear deterrence to climate deals. About 150 to 180 hours over six to eight months.

**Access and balance.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. Free courses, videos, lecture notes and Nobel materials cover the core. You need school algebra and a little probability. The path balances mathematical models with experiments showing how real people play, across cultures.

# About game theory

Game theory studies situations where each person's best choice depends on what others choose: firms setting prices, countries negotiating, animals competing, voters, bidders and friends sharing chores.

This path begins with cooperation and the prisoner's dilemma, teaches Nash equilibrium and mixed strategies, covers sequential and repeated games and bargaining, adds hidden information, signalling, mechanism design and matching, explores evolution and cooperation, and ends with deterrence, climate, behavioural experiments and research.

**Trivia**
- In Robert Axelrod's 1980 computer tournaments, the simple strategy “tit for tat”, submitted by Anatol Rapoport, beat far more complex programs.
- Studies of football penalty kicks found players mix their choices much as game theory predicts.
- John Nash's equilibrium idea, set out in a one-page paper in 1950, later won him a Nobel Prize.
- In ultimatum game experiments across many societies, the offers people make and accept vary widely with local culture.

### Timeline
- **1838 · Antoine Augustin Cournot** models competing firms.
- **1913 · Ernst Zermelo** proves a theorem about chess.
- **1928 · John von Neumann** proves the minimax theorem.
- **1944 · Von Neumann and Morgenstern** publish *Theory of Games and Economic Behavior*.
- **1950 · John Nash** defines his equilibrium; the prisoner's dilemma is devised at RAND.
- **1960 · Thomas Schelling** publishes *The Strategy of Conflict*.
- **1962 · Gale and Shapley** solve the stable matching problem.
- **1973 · Maynard Smith and Price** apply game theory to animal conflict.
- **1980 · Robert Axelrod** runs his prisoner's dilemma tournaments.
- **1994 · Nash, Harsanyi and Selten** share the Nobel Prize in economics.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will know game theory the way a strong undergraduate course expects. You will be able to:

- **Model strategic situations:** players, strategies and payoffs.
- **Find equilibria:** dominant strategies, Nash equilibria and mixed strategies.
- **Analyse dynamic games:** backward induction, subgame perfection, repetition and bargaining.
- **Handle information:** Bayesian games, signalling, mechanism design and matching.
- **Apply and question:** evolution, cooperation, real experiments and policy uses.
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check.

These goals are a draft standard. Economists, mathematicians and political scientists from more than one country will confirm them before the path is published.
