# Aerospace Engineering

How aircraft and spacecraft are designed to fly: aerodynamics and lift, jet and rocket propulsion, lightweight structures, stability, control and orbital mechanics, accident investigation and safety, and the future of cleaner flight. About 250 to 300 hours over ten to twelve months.

**Access and balance.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. NASA's free guides, university courses and official accident reports cover much of the core. You need school physics and algebra; calculus helps from Stage 2. The Space exploration path covers missions and history; this path focuses on engineering.

# About aerospace engineering

Aerospace engineering designs aircraft, rockets and spacecraft. It combines aerodynamics, propulsion, structures, materials, control and systems engineering under strict demands for low weight and high safety.

This path begins with the Wright brothers and simple simulations, explains lift and high-speed flight, covers jet and rocket engines, studies structures and the fatigue lessons of early jets, teaches stability, control and orbits, examines how accidents are investigated, and ends with sustainable aviation and research.

**Trivia**
- The Wright brothers' first flight in 1903 covered about 37 metres, less than the wingspan of a Boeing 747.
- The popular “equal transit time” explanation of lift, that air over the top must rejoin air underneath, is wrong; NASA explains why.
- The first jet aircraft flight was the German Heinkel He 178 in August 1939.
- Concorde could fly from London to New York in under three and a half hours.

### Timeline
- **1783 · The Montgolfier brothers** launch crewed hot-air balloon flights.
- **1799 · George Cayley** sets out the concept of the modern fixed-wing aircraft.
- **1903 · The Wright brothers** make the first powered, controlled aeroplane flights.
- **1915 · NACA** is founded to research aeronautics.
- **1939 · The Heinkel He 178** makes the first jet-powered flight.
- **1947 · The Bell X-1** breaks the sound barrier.
- **1954 · De Havilland Comet crashes** reveal metal fatigue.
- **1957 · Sputnik 1** becomes the first artificial satellite.
- **1969 · Concorde** flies for the first time; Apollo 11 lands on the Moon.
- **1988 · The Airbus A320** enters service with digital fly-by-wire controls.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will know aerospace engineering the way a strong first two years of a degree expect. You will be able to:

- **Explain flight:** lift, drag, airfoils, compressibility and shock waves.
- **Explain propulsion:** jet engines and rockets, thrust and efficiency.
- **Design light structures:** materials, loads, fatigue and composites.
- **Analyse motion:** stability, control, fly-by-wire and orbital transfers.
- **Engineer safely and sustainably:** accident investigation, certification and cleaner flight.
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check.

These goals are a draft standard. Aerospace engineers and engineering educators from more than one country will confirm them before the path is published.

**How stages work.** Each work ends with a short quiz. Score 70% or more across a stage's quizzes to pass it. If you fall short, you can retake the quizzes, or continue anyway: the stage then stays marked **Not finished** until you come back and pass it.

# Final mastery check

Finishing all six stages makes you a capable student of aerospace engineering. To earn the **Ascent Mastery: Aerospace Engineering** certificate, you also complete:

1. **The grand quiz.** 40 game-style questions across all six stages. Pass mark 80%, with unlimited retakes.
2. **Problem set.** Solve unseen lift, thrust, structures and orbit problems.
3. **Design study.** Sketch and size a small aircraft or mission, with its main trade-offs.
4. **Capstone report.** 2,500 words on one of the Stage 6 questions. Strong reports are read by an expert reviewer.
5. **Explain it.** Answer three unseen questions as if to a friend. For example: “How does a 400-tonne aircraft stay in the air?”

The certificate needs every stage passed. It is not a professional engineering or pilot qualification.
