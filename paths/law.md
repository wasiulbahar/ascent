# Law

From why law matters to everyone to how lawyers reason, how the world's legal traditions differ, and the arguments about what law is. About 250 to 300 hours over ten to twelve months.

**Access and balance.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. Many primary sources, such as constitutions, treaties and judgments, are free. This path teaches knowledge about law across the world's traditions, not the law of one country: **it is not legal advice and not a qualification to practise**. For a legal problem, see a qualified lawyer where you live.

# About law

Law is the system of rules a society treats as binding, together with the institutions that make, interpret and enforce them: legislatures, courts, police and lawyers. Studying law means learning how legal reasoning works, how rules differ between traditions, and the deep questions behind them: what makes a rule law, and what makes law just.

This path begins with books that show why law matters, teaches how to study law and introduces the world's legal traditions, covers the main branches through primary sources, explores rights and justice across traditions, reaches the classic theory taught at university, and ends with the arguments about law's purpose and its future.

**Trivia**
- The Code of Hammurabi, carved in Babylon around 1754 BCE, is one of the oldest surviving written law codes.
- The Universal Declaration of Human Rights of 1948 has been translated into more than 500 languages.
- Most of the world's countries use either the civil-law tradition, built on codes, or the common-law tradition, built on judges' decisions; many mix these with religious or customary law.
- In 1992 Australia's High Court rejected the doctrine that the land had belonged to no one before colonisation, in a case brought by Eddie Mabo.

### Timeline
- **c. 1754 BCE · Hammurabi** One of the oldest surviving written law codes.
- **c. 530s CE · Justinian's Code** Roman law collected; the root of the civil-law tradition.
- **8th–10th centuries · Islamic jurisprudence** The great schools of Islamic law form.
- **1215 · Magna Carta** Limits on the English king's power.
- **1804 · The Napoleonic Code** A model civil code copied around the world.
- **1945–46 · Nuremberg** Leaders tried for war crimes and crimes against humanity.
- **1948 · The Universal Declaration of Human Rights**.
- **2002 · The International Criminal Court** begins its work.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will know law the way a strong first year of a university law degree expects, across traditions. You will be able to:

- **Explain the rule of law** and why it matters.
- **Compare legal traditions:** civil, common, Islamic, customary and others.
- **Read primary sources:** a constitution, a treaty and a court judgment.
- **Explain the main branches:** constitutional, criminal, contract, international and human rights law.
- **Weigh the debates:** what law is (Hart, Dworkin, Fuller), and whether it should serve efficiency, justice or equality.
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check.

These goals are a draft standard. Legal scholars from more than one legal tradition will confirm them before the path is published. Knowledge only: never a licence to practise.

5. **Explain it.** Answer three unseen questions as if to a curious friend. For example: "Why should a government obey its own courts?"

The certificate needs every stage passed, and is knowledge only: never a licence to practise law.
