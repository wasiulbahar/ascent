# Mathematics

From puzzles and great stories to proving theorems yourself. About 300 to 400 hours over twelve to eighteen months.

**Access and balance.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. Many of the best courses and even whole textbooks are free. Mathematics is learned with a pencil: from Stage 2 the quizzes include problems, and you can type the number alone. The path also tells mathematics as a world story, from Euclid's Alexandria to Brahmagupta, Madhava and Ramanujan in India.

# About mathematics

Mathematics is the study of pattern, quantity, shape and structure, by pure reasoning. A mathematical proof, once checked, stays true forever: Euclid's proof that there are infinitely many prime numbers is as good today as it was 2,300 years ago. Yet the same pure reasoning runs the modern world, from the encryption on your phone to the models behind weather forecasts.

This path begins with stories and puzzles, then teaches you to think and prove like a mathematician, builds the core of a university degree (calculus, linear algebra, probability, analysis and algebra), and ends with the arguments about what mathematics is, and the great problems still unsolved.

**Trivia**
- The rules for calculating with zero as a number were first written down by the Indian mathematician Brahmagupta in 628 CE.
- Srinivasa Ramanujan, largely self-taught in south India, sent a letter full of astonishing formulas to G. H. Hardy at Cambridge in 1913. Hardy brought him to England.
- The young Carl Friedrich Gauss is said to have added the numbers 1 to 100 in seconds, by pairing them: 1 + 100, 2 + 99, and so on, giving 50 × 101 = 5050.
- Fermat's Last Theorem, scribbled in a book margin in 1637, was proved by Andrew Wiles only in the 1990s.
- In 2006 Grigori Perelman turned down the Fields Medal, the highest prize in mathematics, for proving the Poincaré conjecture.

### Timeline
- **c. 1800 BCE · Babylon** Clay tablets show Pythagorean triples and the square root of 2 to six decimal places.
- **c. 300 BCE · Euclid's Elements** Geometry built from axioms by proof, in Alexandria.
- **628 · Brahmagupta** Rules for zero and negative numbers, in India.
- **c. 820 · Al-Khwarizmi** His book on solving equations gives us the word "algebra"; his name gives us "algorithm".
- **c. 1400 · Madhava** The Kerala school finds infinite series for π, centuries before Europe.
- **1680s · Calculus** Newton and Leibniz independently develop calculus.
- **1736 · Euler** The bridges of Königsberg puzzle starts graph theory.
- **1931 · Gödel** Some true statements can never be proved within a system.
- **1994 · Wiles** Fermat's Last Theorem is proved, 357 years after it was posed.
- **2003 · Perelman** The Poincaré conjecture is proved.

# Before you start

You need only school arithmetic to begin. The path then teaches the rest, rung by rung:

- **Stage 1:** no prerequisites; just curiosity.
- **Stage 2:** school algebra, functions and trigonometry (Khan Academy fills gaps), plus logic and proof.
- **Stage 3:** calculus and linear algebra, taught from the start.
- **Stages 4 and 5:** comfort writing proofs, which Stage 2 builds.

Already know some mathematics? Take the **placement test** on this path's page to skip the stages you have mastered.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will know mathematics the way a strong first and second year of a university mathematics degree expects. You will be able to:

- **Solve problems** in calculus, linear algebra and probability, and explain your method.
- **Write proofs:** direct proof, contradiction, contrapositive and induction, and read proofs critically.
- **Know the core structures:** the real numbers and limits (analysis), groups (algebra), and the first ideas of topology.
- **Tell the history of mathematics as a world story,** including the Greek, Indian and Islamic traditions.
- **Understand the debates:** whether mathematics is discovered or invented, and whether proofs are ever final (Lakatos).
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check.

These goals are a draft standard. Mathematicians from more than one field, and a mathematics teacher, will confirm them before the path is published.

**How stages work.** Each work ends with a short quiz. Score 70% or more across a stage's quizzes to pass it. If you fall short, you can retake the quizzes, or continue anyway: the stage then stays marked **Not finished** until you come back and pass it.
