# Economics

From the stories behind your shopping bill to the arguments that divide central banks. About 220 to 260 hours over nine to twelve months.

**Access and balance.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. Economics has rival schools that read the same facts very differently. This path gives each one in its strongest form, with voices from Europe, the Americas, South Asia and the Global South, and teaches you to check claims against real data. Ascent takes no side.

# About economics

Economics is the study of how people, businesses and governments decide what to do with limited resources, and what happens when millions of those decisions meet. Why do prices rise? Why are some countries rich and others poor? Why do crises happen, and what should governments do about them? Economists answer with models, data and, often, fierce disagreement.

It has a reputation for graphs and jargon. In fact its history is full of big personalities and high stakes: a Scottish philosopher watching pin factories, a German exile writing in a London library, an English economist redesigning the world's money after a war, an Indian child who saw a famine and spent his life asking why. This path starts with the stories, then the toolkit, then the great arguments, and ends with you reading the evidence yourself.

**Trivia**
- Adam Smith's famous "invisible hand" appears only once in *The Wealth of Nations*.
- In 2010 the *Planet Money* podcast bought a "toxic asset", a slice of a failed mortgage bond, to show listeners how the 2008 crisis worked.
- The economists Friedrich Hayek and Gunnar Myrdal, who held almost opposite views, shared the Nobel prize in 1974.
- Elinor Ostrom, a political scientist, became the first woman to win the economics Nobel, in 2009, for showing how communities manage shared resources without either a market or a state.
- Amartya Sen saw the Bengal famine of 1943 as a nine-year-old boy. His later work showed that famines are usually caused by collapses in people's ability to buy food, not simply by too little food.

### Timeline of ideas
- **1776 · Adam Smith** *The Wealth of Nations*: the division of labour, markets and the "invisible hand".
- **1817 · David Ricardo** Comparative advantage: why countries gain from trade even when one is better at everything.
- **1848–1867 · Karl Marx** *The Communist Manifesto* and *Capital*: capitalism's dynamism, and its conflicts.
- **1870s · The marginal revolution** Value explained by what one more unit is worth to someone; the birth of modern microeconomics.
- **1936 · John Maynard Keynes** *The General Theory*: why economies can get stuck in slumps, and what governments can do.
- **1944 · Bretton Woods and Hayek** A new international monetary system; *The Road to Serfdom* warns against central planning.
- **1962–1976 · Milton Friedman** Monetarism and the Chicago school: money, markets and limited government.
- **1979 · Behavioural economics** Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory shows real people don't decide the way textbook models assume.
- **1990s–2000s · Development and institutions** Sen on capabilities, Ostrom on the commons, and randomised trials in the fight against poverty.
- **2008 · The global financial crisis** The biggest crash since the 1930s shakes the profession and revives old debates.
- **Today** Inequality, climate, technology and the role of the state are where the arguments are hottest.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will know economics the way a good university introduction and intermediate sequence expects. You will be able to:

- **Use the core toolkit:** supply and demand, opportunity cost, markets and their failures, GDP, inflation, unemployment, money and interest rates.
- **Explain the main schools of thought** in their strongest form, and name who holds each: classical, Marxian, Keynesian, Austrian, monetarist, behavioural, institutional and development economics.
- **Read the founding texts** in the original: Smith, Marx, Keynes, Hayek and Friedman.
- **Analyse a real event** (a crisis, inflation, a trade dispute) through at least two schools, and say where they disagree.
- **Use real data** from free public sources to check a claim.
- **Have read at least five major economists and critics,** including one who argues the mainstream got it wrong (Keen).
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check.

These goals are a draft standard. Expert reviewers from more than one school of thought will confirm them before the path is published.

## The Wealth of Nations (selections)
- id: e6 · type: read · creator: Adam Smith · year: 1776 · length: about 4 hours for the selections
- edition: Book I, chapters 1 to 3, and Book IV, chapter 2.
- access: free | free on Project Gutenberg

### Introduction
A Scottish philosopher explains why some nations grow rich. His famous example is a pin factory: ten workers, each doing one small step, can make far more pins than ten people each making whole pins alone. From the division of labour, Smith builds a picture of markets that coordinate millions of people without anyone being in charge.

**Trivia**

### Analyse
- **Debate:** was Smith a champion of free markets, or a moral philosopher who feared merchants' power? He warned that business people rarely meet "but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public."

# Stage 3: The Great Debates
level: Reader
intent: Marx, Keynes, Hayek and Friedman, each in their own words.
gate: Score 70% or more across this stage's quizzes.
covers: macroeconomics, political-economy, economic-history

Most of the economic arguments you hear today were first made by four people. Marx saw capitalism as dynamic but driven by conflict. Keynes argued that economies can get stuck, and governments must help. Hayek replied that planners can never know enough to run an economy. Friedman argued for stable money and limited government. Read each in their own words, and decide who is most convincing on which question.

## The Communist Manifesto and Capital, Volume I (selections)
- id: e9 · type: read · creator: Karl Marx (the *Manifesto* with Friedrich Engels) · year: 1848 and 1867 · length: about 8 hours for the selections
- edition: the *Manifesto* in full; from *Capital*, chapter 1 and chapter 10, "The Working Day".
- access: free | both are free online at the Marxists Internet Archive and on Project Gutenberg

### Introduction
No critic of capitalism has been more influential. In the *Manifesto*, Marx and Engels describe capitalism as the most revolutionary force in history, then predict its downfall. ## The General Theory (chapters 1, 12 and 24)
- id: e10 · type: read · creator: John Maynard Keynes · year: 1936 · length: about 5 hours for these chapters
- access: free-and-paid | free online in countries where Keynes's works are now public domain (from 2017 under the life-plus-70-years rule), but not yet in the United States; Robert Skidelsky's *Keynes: A Very Short Introduction* (2010) is a paid guide

### Introduction
Written in the Great Depression, when millions were out of work and the old theory said markets would fix themselves. Keynes argued they might not: if everyone spends less at once, demand collapses and an economy can stay stuck. Governments, he argued, should spend to fill the gap. His ideas shaped economic policy for decades, and came roaring back in 2008.

**Trivia**
- His most famous line, "In the long run we are all dead", comes from an earlier book, *A Tract on Monetary Reform* (1923).
- Chapter 12 compares the stock market to a beauty contest, and speaks of investors' "animal spirits".
- He helped design the post-war international monetary system at the Bretton Woods conference in 1944.

## Thinking, Fast and Slow
- id: e14 · type: read · creator: Daniel Kahneman · year: 2011 · length: about 15 hours
- access: paid | buy, or borrow from a library

### Introduction
We think in two ways: a fast, automatic "System 1" and a slow, careful "System 2". **Trivia**

### Analyse
