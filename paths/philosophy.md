# Philosophy

From a man on trial for asking questions to the debates that fill philosophy journals today. About 220 to 260 hours over nine to twelve months.

**Access and balance.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. Many classics are free in older public-domain translations; for harder texts a modern translation with notes is recommended. Philosophy did not begin or end in Europe: this path gives a full stage to Chinese, Indian and Islamic traditions, and ends with the argument about whose philosophy gets taught.

# About philosophy

The word comes from Greek, *philosophia*: the love of wisdom. Philosophy asks the questions that sit underneath every other subject. What can I know for certain? What makes an action right? Is the mind just the brain? What do we owe each other? Is there any meaning to life? Other subjects can answer questions with experiments or archives; philosophy answers them with arguments, and then argues about the arguments.

It has a reputation for being dry. In fact it began with a trial and a death sentence, and its history is full of exiles, banned books, emperors, revolutionaries and people who changed their minds completely. This path starts with the stories and the big questions, then teaches you to read the great texts, and ends with you making arguments of your own.

**Trivia**
- Socrates, the most famous philosopher of all, never wrote anything. We know him mainly through his student Plato, and through a comic play by Aristophanes, *The Clouds*, that makes fun of him.
- Diogenes the Cynic is said to have lived in a large storage jar in the marketplace of Athens, to show how little a person needs.
- A legend says the philosopher Immanuel Kant took his afternoon walk so punctually that his neighbours in Königsberg set their clocks by it.
- Ludwig Wittgenstein, one of the most influential philosophers of the twentieth century, gave away a huge inherited fortune and worked for some years as a village schoolteacher in Austria.
- The Roman emperor Marcus Aurelius wrote his famous book of philosophy as private notes to himself, partly while on military campaign.

### Timeline of ideas
- **c. 500 BCE · Confucius and the early Greeks** In China, Confucius teaches about virtue and good government; in Greece, thinkers ask what the world is made of.
- **399 BCE · The death of Socrates** Athens tries and executes Socrates for impiety and corrupting the young.
- **c. 387 BCE · Plato's Academy** Plato founds a school in Athens; his student Aristotle later founds his own, the Lyceum.
- **c. 400 BCE – 200 CE · The Bhagavad Gita** The great Indian dialogue on duty and action takes shape within the *Mahabharata*.
- **161–180 CE · Marcus Aurelius** A Stoic philosopher rules the Roman Empire.
- **c. 1100 · Al-Ghazali** In Baghdad, a famous professor gives up his post in a crisis of doubt, and writes about how he found certainty again.
- **1641 · Descartes** *Meditations*: doubt everything, and see what survives.
- **1748–1785 · The Enlightenment** Hume questions cause and effect; Kant builds a morality on reason alone.
- **1859–1861 · Mill** Liberty and happiness: the harm principle and utilitarianism.
- **1887 · Nietzsche** Where do our values come from, and whom do they serve?
- **1949–1971 · Beauvoir, Wittgenstein, Rawls** Gender, language and justice transform the field after the Second World War.
- **Today** Philosophy of mind, ethics of technology, climate and AI, and a growing push to teach the world's philosophical traditions, not one.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will know philosophy the way a good university introduction and second-year sequence expects. You will be able to:

- **Read the core texts** in translation, from Plato and Confucius to Rawls and Wittgenstein: more than twenty works across 2,400 years.
- **Explain the main positions** in the four great fields: ethics, political philosophy, knowledge (epistemology) and reality and mind (metaphysics).
- **Know traditions beyond Europe:** Confucian, Daoist, Indian and Islamic philosophy, and how they answer the same questions differently.
- **Analyse an argument:** spot its premises, test whether the conclusion follows, and find the strongest objection.
- **Have read at least five major historians and critics of philosophy,** including one who argues the field has been too narrow (Van Norden).
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check.

These goals are a draft standard. Expert reviewers will confirm them against university coverage before the path is published.

## The Apology
- id: p1 · type: read · creator: Plato · year: c. 390 BCE · length: about 1 hour
- edition: Benjamin Jowett's translation (free), or G. M. A. Grube's, in *Five Dialogues* (Hackett).
- access: free-and-paid | Jowett's translation is free on Project Gutenberg; Grube is paid

### Introduction
In 399 BCE, the seventy-year-old Socrates stands before a jury of his fellow Athenians, accused of not believing in the city's gods and of corrupting the young. Instead of begging for mercy, he explains why he spent his life questioning people, and why he will not stop. It is short, defiant and moving, and it is where philosophy as a way of life begins.

**Trivia**
- The jury had about 500 citizens, chosen by lot.
- Asked to propose his own punishment, Socrates first suggested that the city should give him free meals for life in the town hall, as an honour.
- The *Apology* contains his most famous line: "the unexamined life is not worth living."
- "Apology" here means a defence speech, not saying sorry.

### Understand
- **Context:** Athens had recently lost a long war with Sparta and lived through a brutal oligarchy. Some of Socrates' former companions were associated with that regime, which may help explain the hostility.
- **Reading guide:** the speech in three parts: his defence; his counter-proposal after the guilty verdict; his last words after the death sentence.
- **Key terms:** the Socratic method (questioning); the oracle at Delphi; *daimonion*, his inner voice.

### Analyse
- **Theme:** why wisdom begins with knowing what you don't know. Socrates says the oracle called him the wisest man because he alone knew that he knew nothing.
- **Debate:** is this Socrates' real speech, or Plato's portrait of an ideal philosopher? Scholars disagree on how much is history.

## Meditations (Books 2 and 4)
- id: p2 · type: read · creator: Marcus Aurelius · year: c. 170–180 CE · length: about 2 hours
- edition: George Long's translation (free), or Gregory Hays's (Modern Library, 2002) for modern English.
- access: free-and-paid | George Long's translation is free on Project Gutenberg; Hays is paid

### Introduction
The most powerful man in the world writes notes to himself at night: be patient with difficult people, don't waste time on what others think, remember that everything passes. Marcus Aurelius was Roman emperor and a follower of Stoicism, a philosophy of calm, duty and self-control. His notebook has become one of the most read books of philosophy in the world.

**Trivia**
- He wrote in Greek, not Latin. The book's original title is usually given as *To Himself*.
- Parts of it were written while he was on military campaign along the Danube.
- He never meant it to be published.

### Understand
- **Key ideas:** focus on what is in your control; judge your judgements, not events; act for the common good.
- **Reading guide:** dip in; each section is a separate thought. Books 2 and 4 are a good start.

## The Republic (Books 1, 2 and 7)
- id: p5 · type: read · creator: Plato · year: c. 375 BCE · length: about 8 hours for these books
- edition: Jowett's translation (free), or G. M. A. Grube revised by C. D. C. Reeve (Hackett, 1992).
- access: free-and-paid | Jowett's translation is free on Project Gutenberg; Grube-Reeve is paid

### Introduction
What is justice, and is a just person happier than an unjust one? Socrates argues with friends and opponents over one long night. On the way, Plato imagines a perfect city ruled by philosophers, and tells the story of prisoners chained in a cave who mistake shadows for reality.

**Trivia**
- In Book 2, Glaucon tells the story of the Ring of Gyges, which makes its wearer invisible. Would anyone stay just if they could never be caught?
- The allegory of the cave, in Book 7, is probably the most quoted image in the history of philosophy.
- Plato's ideal city is ruled by "philosopher-kings", and bans most poets.

### Understand
- **Reading guide:** Book 1: Thrasymachus claims justice is just the advantage of the stronger. Book 2: the challenge of Gyges, and the start of the ideal city. Book 7: the cave and the education of rulers.
- **Key terms:** the Forms (perfect, unchanging realities behind appearances); the philosopher-king.

### Analyse
- **Debate:** utopia or blueprint for tyranny? Karl Popper, in *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945), attacked Plato's city as totalitarian; others read it as a thought experiment about the soul, not a political plan.

### Scholar
- Julia Annas, *An Introduction to Plato's Republic* (Oxford University Press, 1981).
- Essay question: does Socrates answer the challenge of the Ring of Gyges?

## Meditations on First Philosophy
- id: p6 · type: read · creator: René Descartes · year: 1641 · length: about 4 hours
- edition: John Cottingham's translation (Cambridge University Press) is the standard.
- access: free-and-paid | older translations are free on Project Gutenberg; Cottingham is paid

### Introduction
Descartes decides to doubt everything that could possibly be false: his senses, his body, even mathematics, in case an evil demon is deceiving him. Is there anything left? Yes: while he is doubting, he must exist. From that single certainty he tries to rebuild all knowledge. Six short meditations that began modern philosophy.

**Trivia**
- The famous phrase "I think, therefore I am" appears in his earlier *Discourse on the Method* (1637). In the *Meditations* he puts it as "I am, I exist".
- He is said to have died in Stockholm in 1650 of pneumonia, after giving philosophy lessons to Queen Christina of Sweden at five in the morning.
- In the second meditation he uses a piece of melting wax to ask how we really know what anything is.

### Analyse
- **Debate:** the "Cartesian circle." Critics since his own time have objected that Descartes uses God to guarantee clear ideas, and clear ideas to prove God.
- **Key terms:** methodical doubt; the cogito; mind-body dualism.

This stage gives you the three great theories of ethics, side by side: Aristotle's virtue, Kant's duty, and Mill's happiness. Between them comes Hume, the Scottish sceptic who asked how we can know anything about cause and effect, and who, Kant said, woke him from his "dogmatic slumber". By the end you'll be able to take any moral question and say how each would answer it.

## Nicomachean Ethics (Books 1 and 2)
- id: p9 · type: read · creator: Aristotle · year: c. 340 BCE · length: about 5 hours for these books
- edition: Terence Irwin's (Hackett) or Roger Crisp's (Cambridge, 2000) translation.
- access: free-and-paid | older translations are free on Project Gutenberg; Irwin and Crisp are paid

### Introduction
What is a good life? Aristotle's answer is *eudaimonia*: flourishing, living well as a human being. We get there not by following rules but by building good character through practice, until courage, generosity and honesty become second nature. Two short books that founded virtue ethics.

**Trivia**
- Aristotle was Plato's student for about twenty years, and later the tutor of the young Alexander the Great.
- His idea of virtue as a "mean" puts courage between cowardice and recklessness.

### Understand
- **Key terms:** *eudaimonia* (flourishing); virtue; the golden mean; habit.
- **Reading guide:** Book 1: what the highest good is. Book 2: how virtue is learned, and the doctrine of the mean.

## An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding
- id: p10 · type: read · creator: David Hume · year: 1748 · length: about 6 hours
- edition: Peter Millican's edition (Oxford World's Classics).
- access: free-and-paid | free on Project Gutenberg; Millican's annotated edition is paid

### Introduction
We see one billiard ball hit another and the second one moves. But do we ever actually see a cause? Hume, a Scottish philosopher, argues that all we see is one thing following another, again and again, and that our belief that the future will be like the past has no rational proof. It is a short, witty book that unsettled philosophy for good.

**Trivia**
- Immanuel Kant wrote that Hume interrupted his "dogmatic slumber".
- The book ends by telling readers to commit books of empty speculation "to the flames".

### Analyse
- **Theme:** the problem of induction: why expect the sun to rise tomorrow?
- **Debate:** is Hume a destructive sceptic, or a naturalist who explains how belief really works?

## Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals
- id: p11 · type: read · creator: Immanuel Kant · year: 1785 · length: about 5 hours
- edition: Mary Gregor's translation, revised by Jens Timmermann (Cambridge University Press).
- access: free-and-paid | older translations are free on Project Gutenberg; Gregor-Timmermann is paid

### Introduction
Kant argues that morality can't depend on results or feelings, only on reason. His test, the categorical imperative: act only on a rule you could will everyone to follow, and never treat people merely as a means to your own ends. Demanding and difficult, but perhaps the most influential short book in ethics.

**Trivia**
- Kant spent his whole life in and around Königsberg, a city on the Baltic that is now Kaliningrad in Russia.
- One of his examples is a lying promise: could you will that everyone made promises they meant to break?

### Analyse
- **Debate:** must you tell the truth even to a murderer at the door asking where your friend is hiding? Kant said yes, in a later essay, "On a Supposed Right to Lie" (1797). Most readers think this goes too far; Kantians argue over whether his theory really requires it.

## Utilitarianism and On Liberty
- id: p12 · type: read · creator: John Stuart Mill · year: 1861 and 1859 · length: about 8 hours
- access: free | both are free on Project Gutenberg

### Introduction
Two short books that shaped modern liberal societies. *Utilitarianism* argues that the right action is the one that produces the greatest happiness. *On Liberty* argues that the only good reason to restrict anyone's freedom is to prevent harm to others. Mill wrote both with his partner and later wife, Harriet Taylor, whom he credited as a co-author of his thinking.

**Trivia**
- Mill's father started teaching him Greek when he was three.
- Mill wrote that it is "better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied."

### Analyse
- **Debate:** can you add up happiness? Critics argue utilitarianism would allow terrible things to a few if they made many happy. Mill tried to answer this with his theory of rights and higher pleasures.

Philosophy was born more than once. While Socrates questioned the Athenians, Confucius was teaching in China, and the questions of Indian philosophy were already old. ## The Analects (selections)
- id: p13 · type: read · creator: Confucius, compiled by his followers · year: c. 5th–3rd century BCE · length: about 4 hours
- edition: Edward Slingerland's translation with commentary (Hackett, 2003).
- access: free-and-paid | James Legge's 1861 translation is free online; Slingerland is paid

### Introduction
Short sayings and conversations of Confucius, a teacher in ancient China, collected by his students after his death. His questions are practical: how to become a good person, how to treat family and friends, how rulers should govern. **Trivia**

- One saying is a version of the golden rule: do not do to others what you would not want done to yourself.

### Understand
- **Key terms:** *ren* (humaneness, goodness); *li* (ritual and proper conduct); *junzi* (the exemplary person).

## Tao Te Ching
- id: p14 · type: read · creator: attributed to Laozi · year: c. 4th century BCE · length: about 2 hours
- edition: D. C. Lau's translation (Penguin).
- access: free-and-paid | James Legge's translation is free on Project Gutenberg; Lau is paid

### Introduction
Eighty-one short poems about the Dao, the way of things, which cannot be put into words. Where Confucius stresses effort and ritual, the *Tao Te Ching* praises softness, emptiness and *wu wei*, acting without forcing. Its favourite image is water: the softest thing, which wears away the hardest stone.

**Trivia**
- It is one of the most translated books in the world.
- Whether Laozi was a real person is uncertain. His name means "Old Master".
- A later Daoist, Zhuangzi, wrote the famous story of dreaming he was a butterfly, and waking unsure whether he was a man who had dreamed of being a butterfly, or a butterfly dreaming of being a man.

## Bhagavad Gita
- id: p15 · type: read · creator: part of the *Mahabharata* · year: between about 400 BCE and 200 CE (dates debated) · length: about 3 hours
- edition: Eknath Easwaran's translation (Nilgiri Press), or Laurie Patton's (Penguin, 2008).
- access: free-and-paid | Edwin Arnold's 1885 verse translation is free on Project Gutenberg; modern translations are paid

### Introduction
On the edge of a great battle, the warrior Arjuna loses his nerve: he cannot bring himself to fight his own relatives. His charioteer, the god Krishna, answers him in a dialogue about duty, action and the self. The Gita's central idea is to act rightly without being attached to the results of your actions.

**Trivia**
- It has 18 chapters and 700 verses.
- The battle takes place on the field of Kurukshetra.
- After the first atomic bomb test in 1945, J. Robert Oppenheimer recalled a line from the Gita: "Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds."

### Analyse
- **Debate:** does the Gita justify war, or use the battle as a symbol of an inner struggle? Gandhi, who read it daily, took the second view in his commentary *Anasaktiyoga* (1929).

### Quiz
**Multiple choice.** Who are the two speakers of the Bhagavad Gita?
- Rama and Sita

# Stage 5: The Modern Turn
level: Specialist
intent: Four books that changed philosophy after the nineteenth century.
gate: Score 70% or more across this stage's quizzes.

These are the books that made philosophy modern: Nietzsche asking where our values come from, Beauvoir asking what it means to be a woman, Rawls asking what a fair society would look like, and the later Wittgenstein asking what words really do. They are harder going, but by now you have the tools to read them.

## On the Genealogy of Morality
- id: p17 · type: read · creator: Friedrich Nietzsche · year: 1887 · length: about 6 hours
- edition: Maudemarie Clark and Alan Swensen's translation (Hackett, 1998).
- access: free-and-paid | Horace Samuel's 1913 translation is free on Project Gutenberg; Clark-Swensen is paid

### Introduction
Where do our ideas of good and evil come from? **Trivia**

- Nietzsche's sister later edited his work to suit her own nationalist and antisemitic politics. Scholars have spent decades undoing the damage.
