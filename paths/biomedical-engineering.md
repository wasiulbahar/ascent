# Biomedical Engineering

How engineering heals: biomechanics, implants and biomaterials, tissue engineering, medical imaging, life-saving devices and their hidden biases, neural and rehabilitation engineering, and regulation, global health and safety. About 200 to 250 hours over eight to ten months.

**Access and balance.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. Free WHO and regulator resources, Nobel materials and university explainers cover much of the core. You need school biology, physics and maths; the Anatomy and physiology path helps. The path covers devices designed for every setting, including clinics with little equipment or power.

# About biomedical engineering

Biomedical engineering applies engineering to medicine and biology: artificial joints and heart valves, imaging scanners, pacemakers and dialysis machines, prosthetic limbs, and engineered tissues.

This path starts with the field's stories and devices, studies biomechanics, biomaterials and tissue engineering, explains X-ray, CT, MRI and ultrasound imaging, covers life-saving devices and the biases they can carry, explores neural and rehabilitation engineering, and ends with regulation, design for low-resource settings, safety scandals and research.

**Trivia**
- Arne Larsson, who received the first fully implanted pacemaker in 1958, went on to have 26 pacemakers and lived to 86.
- Godfrey Hounsfield built the first CT scanner at EMI, the company that also released the Beatles' records.
- Willem Kolff built an early dialysis machine during the Second World War using sausage casings and a drum.
- Studies have found pulse oximeters can overestimate blood oxygen in people with darker skin, a bias with life-or-death consequences.

### Timeline
- **1895 · Wilhelm Röntgen** discovers X-rays.
- **1903 · Willem Einthoven** records the electrocardiogram.
- **1943 · Willem Kolff** treats patients with an early artificial kidney.
- **1958 · Rune Elmqvist and Åke Senning** implant the first pacemaker in Sweden.
- **1962 · John Charnley** develops a lasting hip replacement.
- **1971 · The first CT scan** of a patient is taken.
- **1977 · The first human MRI scan** is made.
- **1978 · Graeme Clark** implants a multichannel cochlear implant in Australia.
- **1993 · Langer and Vacanti** define tissue engineering.
- **2006 · A paralysed person** controls a computer cursor with a brain implant.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will understand biomedical engineering the way a strong undergraduate course expects. You will be able to:

- **Apply biomechanics:** forces in the body, joints and implants.
- **Explain biomaterials and tissue engineering:** biocompatibility, scaffolds and cells.
- **Explain medical imaging:** X-ray, CT, MRI and ultrasound physics and trade-offs.
- **Explain devices:** pacemakers, dialysis and monitors, including bias and failure.
- **Engineer responsibly:** regulation, low-resource design, safety and ethics.
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check.

These goals are a draft standard. Biomedical engineers and clinicians from more than one country will confirm them before the path is published.

**How stages work.** Each work ends with a short quiz. Score 70% or more across a stage's quizzes to pass it. If you fall short, you can retake the quizzes, or continue anyway: the stage then stays marked **Not finished** until you come back and pass it.

# Final mastery check

Finishing all six stages makes you a capable student of biomedical engineering. To earn the **Ascent Mastery: Biomedical Engineering** certificate, you also complete:

1. **The grand quiz.** 40 game-style questions across all six stages. Pass mark 80%, with unlimited retakes.
2. **Device analysis.** Analyse a real medical device: how it works, its risks, regulation and who it may fail.
3. **Design project.** Propose a device for a real need, with requirements, testing and safety plans.
4. **Capstone report.** 2,500 words on one of the Stage 6 questions. Strong reports are read by an expert reviewer.
5. **Explain it.** Answer three unseen questions as if to a patient. For example: “How does an MRI see inside me?”

The certificate needs every stage passed. It is not a professional engineering or clinical qualification.
