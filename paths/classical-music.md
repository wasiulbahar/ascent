# Classical music

From the tunes you already half-know to the music that rewired the twentieth century. About 200 to 250 hours of listening and reading over nine to twelve months.

**Listening and access.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. Almost every piece here can be streamed free with adverts on the big streaming services, and most scores are free on IMSLP, the public-domain score library. A specific recommended recording may need a subscription. Listen on headphones or good speakers whenever you can: much of this music lives in quiet details.

# About classical music

"Classical music" is the everyday name for about a thousand years of music written down in Europe and, later, all over the world: plainchant sung in medieval monasteries, the operas of Venice, the symphonies of Vienna, the film scores of today. What ties it together is notation. Composers wrote music on paper precisely enough for strangers to perform it centuries later, which is why a pianist today can play exactly the notes Bach published in 1741.

It can look forbidding from outside: long pieces, unfamiliar names, concert-hall etiquette. Inside, it is the same thing as any music you love: tunes, rhythm, surprise and feeling, built into larger shapes. This path starts with the pieces almost everyone enjoys on first hearing and climbs slowly to music that asks more of you, and gives more back.

**Trivia**
- Mozart wrote his first symphony at eight, in London, in 1764.
- Beethoven was so deaf by the premiere of his Ninth Symphony in 1824 that a singer had to turn him around to see the audience applauding.
- Bach was largely forgotten after his death. A twenty-year-old Felix Mendelssohn revived his *St Matthew Passion* in Berlin in 1829 and started a Bach craze that never ended.
- Haydn's skull was stolen by phrenologists shortly after his burial in 1809. It was only reunited with the rest of his body in 1954.
- A modern symphony orchestra has around 80 to 100 players. In Haydn's time it might have had 30.

### Timeline of the periods
- **c. 500–1400 · Medieval** Plainchant in monasteries; Hildegard of Bingen; the first written polyphony (several melodies at once).
- **c. 1400–1600 · Renaissance** Choral music of great clarity: Josquin, Palestrina, Byrd.
- **1600–1750 · Baroque** Opera is born. Monteverdi, Vivaldi, Handel, Bach. Driving rhythms and ornate lines.
- **1750–1820 · Classical** Balance and clarity: Haydn, Mozart, early Beethoven. The symphony, the string quartet and the piano sonata take shape.
- **1820–1900 · Romantic** Big emotions, big orchestras: Schubert, Chopin, Wagner, Verdi, Tchaikovsky, Brahms, Mahler.
- **1900–today · Modern and contemporary** Rules broken and remade: Debussy, Stravinsky, Schoenberg, Ligeti, Reich, Pärt, and composers writing now.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will know classical music the way a good university music-appreciation and history sequence expects. You will be able to:

- **Recognise the periods by ear,** from medieval chant to minimalism, and name a few composers from each.
- **Know the core repertoire:** about 40 central works, including symphonies, concertos, chamber music, song, choral music and opera.
- **Explain how the main forms work:** sonata form, fugue, theme and variations, the four-movement symphony, the song cycle, the opera.
- **Follow a score** while listening, at a basic level.
- **Know the history and its arguments:** patronage, the concert hall, nationalism, modernism, the "canon" debate, and why some critics loved and others hated the twentieth century's experiments.
- **Have read at least five major writers on music,** including one who argued against the mainstream (Adorno).
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check, including a blind listening test.

These goals are a draft standard. Expert reviewers will confirm them against university coverage before the path is published.

## The Four Seasons: "Spring"
- id: m1 · type: listen · creator: Antonio Vivaldi · year: published 1725 · length: 10 minutes
- edition: any period-instrument recording, for example Il Giardino Armonico (1994), for bright, fast playing.
- access: free-and-paid | free to stream with adverts; the score is free on IMSLP

### Introduction
Three short movements that paint a picture: birds singing, a spring storm, a shepherd asleep with his dog, and a country dance. It is probably the most recognisable opening in Baroque music, and the easiest way to hear how a solo violin and a small string orchestra trade ideas.

**Trivia**
- Vivaldi was a priest with flaming red hair, nicknamed "the Red Priest".
- He wrote much of his music for the girls' orchestra of the Ospedale della Pietà, a Venetian orphanage, whose concerts drew visitors from all over Europe.
- Each of the four concertos came with a sonnet describing its scene, possibly written by Vivaldi himself.

### Understand
- **Form:** a concerto in three movements, fast, slow, fast.
- **Listening guide:** first movement: the bright tutti theme returns between episodes of birdsong (high trills), a murmuring brook and a thunderstorm. Second movement: the violin sings over sleepy strings while the violas imitate a barking dog. Third movement: a lilting country dance.
- **Key terms:** concerto; tutti (everyone plays); ritornello (the returning theme); programme music (music that tells a story or paints a scene).

### Analyse
- **Theme:** can instrumental music really "describe" anything? Vivaldi's sonnets make you hear birds; without them, would you?
- **Debate:** programme music versus "absolute" music became a fierce argument in the nineteenth century. Keep the question in mind until Stage 4.

### Scholar
- Michael Talbot, *Vivaldi* (Master Musicians series, Oxford University Press).
- Essay question: does knowing the sonnets make "Spring" better music, or only easier music?

## Eine kleine Nachtmusik, first movement
- id: m2 · type: listen · creator: Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart · year: 1787 · length: 6 minutes
- edition: any major chamber orchestra or string quartet recording.
- access: free-and-paid | free to stream with adverts; the score is free on IMSLP

### Introduction
Six minutes of pure melody. The opening rocket of a theme is one of the most famous in music, and the movement is a perfect, small example of how a Classical piece states a tune, contrasts it with another, develops them, and brings them home.

**Trivia**
- The title means "a little serenade", literally "a little night music".
- Mozart wrote it in the summer of 1787 while he was also writing the opera *Don Giovanni*.
- It was not published until 1827, decades after his death. Mozart's own catalogue lists a fifth movement that has never been found.

### Understand
- **Form:** sonata form in miniature: exposition (two contrasting themes), development (the themes are taken apart), recapitulation (they return).
- **Listening guide:** the loud, rising opening theme; a gentler second theme; the short development; the return.
- **Key terms:** serenade; sonata form; exposition, development, recapitulation.

### Analyse
- **Theme:** balance. Notice how every phrase seems to answer the one before it. This sense of question and answer is the heart of the Classical style.

### Scholar
- Charles Rosen, *The Classical Style* (1971) explains why this balance matters. You will read it in Stage 4.

## Symphony No. 5, first movement
- id: m3 · type: listen · creator: Ludwig van Beethoven · year: 1808 · length: 7 to 8 minutes
- edition: Carlos Kleiber and the Vienna Philharmonic (Deutsche Grammophon, 1975) is the classic recording.
- access: free-and-paid | free to stream with adverts; the score is free on IMSLP

### Introduction
Four notes, short-short-short-long, and possibly the most famous opening in all music. What makes the movement great is not the motif itself but what Beethoven does with it: the whole movement grows out of those four notes, driving forward with relentless energy.

**Trivia**
- It premiered in Vienna on 22 December 1808 in a freezing hall, at a concert that lasted about four hours and also introduced his Sixth Symphony and Fourth Piano Concerto.
- The story that Beethoven called the opening "fate knocking at the door" comes from his secretary Anton Schindler, who is known to have invented anecdotes.
- In the Second World War, the BBC used the opening rhythm in broadcasts to occupied Europe: in Morse code, short-short-short-long is V, for victory.

### Understand
- **Form:** sonata form, on a much larger and more dramatic scale than the Mozart.
- **Listening guide:** the motif; a horn call introducing the gentler second theme (even there, the four-note rhythm grumbles in the bass); the development; a surprising, lonely oboe solo just as the music returns.
- **Key term:** motif, a short musical idea that a whole piece can be built from.

### Analyse
- **Theme:** how much can be built from how little? Count the motif. It is in almost every bar.
- **Debate:** the symphony is often heard as a journey from struggle (C minor) to triumph (the C major finale). Is that story in the music, or put there by listeners?

### Scholar
- E. T. A. Hoffmann's 1810 review of the symphony, a founding text of Romantic music criticism. In English: *E. T. A. Hoffmann's Musical Writings*, edited by David Charlton (Cambridge University Press, 1989).

## Symphony No. 9 "From the New World"
- id: m5 · type: listen · creator: Antonín Dvořák · year: 1893 · length: about 42 minutes
- edition: Rafael Kubelík and the Berlin Philharmonic (Deutsche Grammophon, 1972), or any major orchestra.
- access: free-and-paid | free to stream with adverts; the score is free on IMSLP

### Introduction
Your first complete symphony: four movements, each with tunes you can hold on to. Dvořák, a Czech composer working in New York, wrote it about his impressions of America, and the slow movement's melody became so famous that many people think it is a folk song.

**Trivia**
- It premiered at Carnegie Hall in New York in December 1893 and was an immediate hit.
- A pupil of Dvořák later set the slow movement's melody to words as the song "Goin' Home", which many people now assume is a traditional spiritual.
- Neil Armstrong is often said to have taken a recording of it to the Moon in 1969.

### Understand
- **Form:** the four-movement symphony: a fast first movement, a slow movement, a dance-like scherzo, a big finale.
- **Listening guide:** listen for the opening movement's main theme returning, transformed, in the finale. Dvořák ties the whole symphony together this way.
- **Key terms:** movement; scherzo; cyclic form (themes returning across movements).

### Analyse
- **Debate:** how "American" is it? Dvořák said he was inspired by African American spirituals and Native American music; critics have argued ever since about how much of that is really in the music and how much is Czech.

## The Nutcracker Suite
- id: m6 · type: listen · creator: Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky · year: 1892 · length: about 22 minutes
- access: free-and-paid | free to stream with adverts; the score is free on IMSLP

### Introduction
Eight short dances from Tchaikovsky's Christmas ballet, each built around a different colour of the orchestra: the tinkling Sugar Plum Fairy, the Russian trepak, the flutes of the Mirlitons, the waltz of the flowers. It is a tour of the orchestra disguised as a party.

**Trivia**
- Tchaikovsky found a new instrument, the celesta, in Paris and kept it secret so that rival composers would not use it before him. You hear it in the "Dance of the Sugar Plum Fairy".
- The suite was performed in March 1892, months before the full ballet premiered in St Petersburg that December. The ballet itself was not a success at first.

### Understand
- **Key terms:** suite (a set of pieces drawn from a larger work); orchestration (choosing which instruments play what).
- **Listening guide:** in each dance, ask which instrument has the tune.

## Piano Concerto No. 2
- id: m9 · type: listen · creator: Sergei Rachmaninoff · year: 1901 · length: about 34 minutes
- edition: Rachmaninoff's own 1929 recording with Leopold Stokowski and the Philadelphia Orchestra, for history; a modern recording for sound quality.
- access: free-and-paid | free to stream with adverts; modern recordings are paid

### Introduction
Huge, yearning melodies over a crashing piano. It is one of the most loved concertos ever written, and it bridges the bright music you know and the deeper music ahead.

**Trivia**
- After his First Symphony failed disastrously in 1897, Rachmaninoff fell into depression and could barely compose. Treatment with the doctor Nikolai Dahl helped him recover, and he dedicated this concerto to Dahl.
- Its slow movement became the soundtrack of David Lean's film *Brief Encounter* (1945).
- The opening chords sound like a bell coming closer; Rachmaninoff spent part of his childhood near Novgorod and loved its church bells; many listeners hear them in these chords.

## Requiem in D minor
- id: m10 · type: listen · creator: Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart, completed by Franz Xaver Süssmayr · year: 1791 · length: about 50 minutes
- edition: John Eliot Gardiner, English Baroque Soloists (1986), on period instruments.
- access: free-and-paid | free to stream with adverts; the score is free on IMSLP

### Introduction
Mozart's last work, a mass for the dead, left unfinished when he died at 35. It holds some of the most powerful choral writing ever composed, from the terror of the "Dies irae" to the eight bars of the "Lacrimosa" he may have been writing in his final days.

**Trivia**
- It was commissioned anonymously by Count Franz von Walsegg, who planned to pass it off as his own composition in memory of his wife.
- After Mozart's death his widow, Constanze, had it completed, mostly by his pupil Süssmayr, so she could collect the fee.
- The film *Amadeus* (1984) suggests that the rival composer Salieri commissioned it. That is fiction.

### Understand
- **Form:** the Latin mass for the dead, in sections: Introitus, Kyrie, Sequence (including "Dies irae", "Tuba mirum", "Lacrimosa"), Offertory, Sanctus, Agnus Dei, Communion.
- **Key term:** requiem.

### Analyse
- **Debate:** how much is really Mozart? Scholars argue over how much Süssmayr completed from Mozart's sketches and how much he composed himself; several modern completions offer alternatives.

## Erlkönig

## Goldberg Variations
- id: m15 · type: listen · creator: Johann Sebastian Bach · year: 1741 · length: about 50 minutes
- edition: Glenn Gould (CBS, 1981) on piano; or a harpsichord recording to hear Bach's own instrument.
- access: free-and-paid | free to stream with adverts; the score is free on IMSLP

### Introduction
A gentle aria, thirty variations on its bass line, and the aria again. Every third variation is a canon, in which one voice chases another at a steadily widening interval. It is the clearest way into how Bach thinks: strict rules producing music of astonishing freedom.

**Trivia**
- An early biography claims Bach wrote it for a sleepless count, whose young harpsichordist, Johann Gottlieb Goldberg, played it to him at night. Most scholars doubt the story.
- Glenn Gould made his name with it in 1955 and recorded it again in 1981, shortly before his death. You can hear him humming along.

### Understand
- **Form:** theme and variations; canon (a melody imitated by another voice, starting later).
- **Listening guide:** follow the bass line, not the tune. Every variation is built on it.
