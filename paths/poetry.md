# Poetry

How to read, hear and love poems: sound, rhythm and image, the great forms and traditions from many languages, poetry in translation, modern and contemporary voices, and how to write about poems yourself. About 250 to 300 hours over ten to twelve months.

**Access and balance.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. The Poetry Foundation, the Academy of American Poets and many out-of-copyright collections are free. The path reads poetry from Asia, Africa, the Middle East, Europe and the Americas, in translation where needed, alongside poetry in English.

# About poetry

Poetry is language shaped by sound, rhythm and image to say what ordinary speech cannot. It is among the oldest arts, found in every culture, from epics and hymns to love songs, protest and spoken word.

This path begins by helping you enjoy poems, teaches the tools of sound, rhythm, form and image, travels through great traditions from many languages, explores modern and contemporary poetry, and ends with translation, criticism and writing about poems yourself.

**Trivia**
- The *Epic of Gilgamesh*, from ancient Mesopotamia, is one of the oldest surviving works of literature, over 4,000 years old in its earliest parts.
- The Japanese haiku traditionally has 17 sound units in a 5–7–5 pattern.
- The ghazal, a form of linked couplets, spread from Arabic into Persian, Urdu, Turkish and, in recent decades, English.
- Rabindranath Tagore became the first non-European to win the Nobel Prize in Literature, in 1913, largely for his poetry.

### Timeline
- **c. 2100 BCE · The earliest Gilgamesh poems** are written in Sumerian.
- **c. 1000–600 BCE · The *Shijing*** China's Classic of Poetry, is compiled.
- **c. 700s BCE · Homer's epics** the *Iliad* and *Odyssey*.
- **8th century · Li Bai and Du Fu** in Tang China.
- **13th century · Rumi** writes in Persian.
- **1600s · Bashō** perfects the haiku.
- **1798 · *Lyrical Ballads*** Wordsworth and Coleridge.
- **1913 · Tagore** wins the Nobel Prize in Literature.
- **1922 · *The Waste Land*** T. S. Eliot.
- **1990s · Spoken-word poetry** spreads worldwide.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will read poetry the way a strong first year of a university literature course expects. You will be able to:

- **Hear a poem:** rhythm, metre, rhyme and sound.
- **Name forms:** sonnet, haiku, ghazal, free verse and more.
- **Read closely:** imagery, metaphor, voice and structure.
- **Place poems in traditions:** from several languages and centuries.
- **Write about a poem:** a clear close reading with evidence.
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check.

These goals are a draft standard. Poets and scholars of poetry from more than one language tradition will confirm them before the path is published.

**How stages work.** Each work ends with a short quiz. Score 70% or more across a stage's quizzes to pass it. If you fall short, you can retake the quizzes, or continue anyway: the stage then stays marked **Not finished** until you come back and pass it.

# Final mastery check

Finishing all six stages makes you a capable reader of poetry. To earn the **Ascent Mastery: Poetry** certificate, you also complete:

1. **The grand quiz.** 40 game-style questions across all six stages. Pass mark 80%, with unlimited retakes.
2. **Close reading.** Write a close reading of an unseen poem.
3. **Forms portfolio.** Write a sonnet, a haiku sequence and a ghazal.
4. **Capstone essay.** 2,500 words on one of the Stage 6 questions. Strong essays are read by an expert reviewer.
5. **Explain it.** Answer three unseen questions as if to a curious friend. For example: “Does a poem have to rhyme?”

The certificate needs every stage passed.
