# Chemical Engineering

How raw materials become fuels, fertilisers, medicines, plastics and clean water at industrial scale: mass and energy balances, thermodynamics, transport phenomena, reactors and separations, process control and design, safety, and sustainable industry. About 250 to 300 hours over ten to twelve months.

**Access and balance.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. Free screencasts, textbooks and open-source simulators cover much of the core, and the classic textbooks are worth borrowing. You need school chemistry, physics and algebra; calculus is needed from Stage 2. Never try industrial processes at home.

# About chemical engineering

Chemical engineering designs and runs processes that transform materials on a large scale, safely and economically. Chemical engineers work in energy, food, pharmaceuticals, water, materials and environmental protection.

This path begins with the stories and products of the field, teaches mass and energy balances and thermodynamics, covers fluid flow, heat and mass transfer, moves to reactors, separations, control and design, studies industrial disasters and safety, and ends with decarbonising industry and current research.

**Trivia**
- Estimates suggest about half the nitrogen in the human body passed through the Haber–Bosch process, via fertiliser and food.
- MIT offered one of the first chemical engineering courses in 1888.
- The idea of “unit operations”, breaking any process into standard steps such as distillation, was named by Arthur D. Little in 1915.
- Scaling penicillin up in deep fermentation tanks during the Second World War turned a laboratory curiosity into a mass-produced medicine.

### Timeline
- **1791 · Nicolas Leblanc** patents a process to make soda ash.
- **1861 · Ernest Solvay** develops a cleaner soda process.
- **1888 · MIT** starts one of the first chemical engineering courses.
- **1908 · The American Institute of Chemical Engineers** is founded.
- **1913 · Haber–Bosch ammonia** goes into industrial production.
- **1915 · Arthur D. Little** names “unit operations”.
- **1922 · The Institution of Chemical Engineers** is founded in Britain.
- **1940s · Deep-tank fermentation** mass-produces penicillin.
- **1960 · Bird, Stewart and Lightfoot** publish *Transport Phenomena*.
- **1984 · The Bhopal gas leak** becomes the world's worst industrial disaster.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will know chemical engineering the way a strong first two to three years of a degree expect. You will be able to:

- **Do balances:** mass and energy balances on processes with reactions and recycles.
- **Apply thermodynamics:** phase equilibrium and energy in real processes.
- **Use transport phenomena:** fluid flow, heat transfer and mass transfer.
- **Design units:** reactors, separations and control loops, and simulate whole processes.
- **Engineer safely and sustainably:** hazard analysis, inherently safer design and decarbonisation.
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check.

These goals are a draft standard. Chemical engineers and engineering educators from more than one country will confirm them before the path is published.

**How stages work.** Each work ends with a short quiz. Score 70% or more across a stage's quizzes to pass it. If you fall short, you can retake the quizzes, or continue anyway: the stage then stays marked **Not finished** until you come back and pass it.

# Final mastery check

Finishing all six stages makes you a capable student of chemical engineering. To earn the **Ascent Mastery: Chemical Engineering** certificate, you also complete:

1. **The grand quiz.** 40 game-style questions across all six stages. Pass mark 80%, with unlimited retakes.
2. **Problem set.** Solve unseen balance, transport and reactor problems.
3. **Design project.** Simulate a small process, size its main units and write a safety review.
4. **Capstone report.** 2,500 words on one of the Stage 6 questions. Strong reports are read by an expert reviewer.
5. **Explain it.** Answer three unseen questions as if to a friend. For example: “How does a fertiliser plant pull nitrogen out of the air?”

The certificate needs every stage passed. It is not a professional engineering qualification.
