# Classics: Greece and Rome

Ancient Greece and Rome through their own words and their modern readers: myth and epic, Athens and democracy, Rome and its empire, the wider ancient world, and why the classics are still argued over. About 250 to 300 hours over ten to twelve months.

**Access and balance.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. Almost every ancient text is free in older translations, and the Perseus Digital Library has the originals. The path reads Greece and Rome as part of a connected ancient world, and includes modern critics of how the classics have been used.

# About classics

Classics studies the languages, literature, history, philosophy, art and archaeology of ancient Greece and Rome, roughly from Homer (around 700 BCE) to the end of the western Roman Empire (476 CE). These cultures gave later worlds epic poetry, tragedy, history writing, philosophy, democracy, republican government and Roman law, and much of their vocabulary.

This path begins with lively modern guides and great translations, reads the myths and epics, studies classical Athens, then Rome, widens to the connected ancient world and the women of myth and history, and ends with how the classics are read, used and challenged today.

**Trivia**
- Emily Wilson's 2017 translation of the *Odyssey* was the first published in English by a woman.
- Athenian democracy chose many officials by lot, not by election.
- Rome's population probably reached around a million people in the first century CE, a size no European city would match again until about 1800.
- By dictionary counts, more than half of English words come from Latin, directly or through French.

### Timeline
- **c. 8th–7th century BCE · Homer's *Iliad* and *Odyssey***
- **509 BCE · The Roman Republic** is founded, by tradition.
- **508 BCE · Cleisthenes' reforms** Athenian democracy begins.
- **490–479 BCE · The Persian Wars**
- **399 BCE · The trial and death of Socrates**
- **336–323 BCE · Alexander the Great** conquers an empire to India.
- **44 BCE · Julius Caesar** is assassinated.
- **27 BCE · Augustus** becomes the first emperor.
- **212 CE · Roman citizenship** is given to almost all free people in the empire.
- **476 CE · The last western Roman emperor** is deposed.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will know classics the way a strong first year of a university classics degree expects (in translation). You will be able to:

- **Read the great texts:** Homer, the tragedians, Herodotus, Plato, Virgil, Ovid and Tacitus, in translation.
- **Explain Greek and Roman history:** from the archaic age to the fall of the western empire.
- **Explain institutions:** Athenian democracy, the Roman Republic and Empire.
- **See the wider world:** Persia, Egypt, Carthage and others, and the people history left out.
- **Weigh the reception:** how the classics have been used, admired and challenged since.
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check.

These goals are a draft standard. Classicists from more than one country will confirm them before the path is published. (Ancient Greek and Latin are optional extras: see Side branches.)

**How stages work.** Each work ends with a short quiz. Score 70% or more across a stage's quizzes to pass it. If you fall short, you can retake the quizzes, or continue anyway: the stage then stays marked **Not finished** until you come back and pass it.
