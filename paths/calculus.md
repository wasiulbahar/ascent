# Calculus

The mathematics of change: functions and limits, derivatives and rates of change, integrals and accumulation, the fundamental theorem, series, differential equations and multivariable calculus, with applications in science, economics and engineering. About 250 to 300 hours over ten to twelve months.

**Access and balance.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. Free textbooks, video courses and practice sites cover everything here. You need school algebra first: if it's rusty, start with Khan Academy's algebra and precalculus. Practice problems matter more than reading.

# About calculus

Calculus studies how things change and how small changes add up. Derivatives measure rates of change, such as speed; integrals measure accumulation, such as distance travelled. Together they describe motion, growth, waves, economies and much of modern science.

This path begins with intuitive introductions, builds functions and limits, teaches derivatives and their uses, then integrals and the fundamental theorem, explores series and differential equations, and ends with multivariable calculus and applications.

**Trivia**
- Isaac Newton and Gottfried Leibniz developed calculus independently in the late 1600s and quarrelled bitterly over who was first.
- The notation dy/dx and the integral sign ∫ come from Leibniz; ∫ is an elongated S for “sum”.
- Indian mathematicians of the Kerala school found infinite series for sine and cosine in the 14th–16th centuries.
- Archimedes used a method close to integration to find the area of a parabolic segment around 250 BCE.

### Timeline
- **c. 250 BCE · Archimedes** uses the method of exhaustion to find areas.
- **c. 1350–1550 · The Kerala school** finds infinite series for trigonometric functions.
- **1665–66 · Isaac Newton** develops his “method of fluxions”.
- **1684 · Leibniz** publishes his calculus.
- **1734 · Bishop Berkeley** attacks infinitesimals as “ghosts of departed quantities”.
- **1748 · Euler's *Introductio*** makes functions central.
- **1821 · Cauchy** puts limits on a firmer footing.
- **1854 · Riemann** defines the integral rigorously.
- **1872 · Weierstrass** completes the epsilon–delta definition.
- **1960s · Robinson's non-standard analysis** revives infinitesimals rigorously.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will know calculus the way a strong first year of a university calculus course expects. You will be able to:

- **Work with functions and limits:** graphs, continuity and limit laws.
- **Differentiate:** rules, chain rule, and applications to rates, slopes and optimisation.
- **Integrate:** antiderivatives, substitution, areas and the fundamental theorem.
- **Use series and differential equations:** Taylor series and simple models of change.
- **Apply calculus:** in physics, biology, economics and engineering.
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check.
