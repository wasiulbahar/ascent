# Zoology

The science of animals: their astonishing diversity, how their bodies work, how they behave and think, how they evolved and interact, the crisis facing wildlife, and how animals and people share the world. About 180 to 220 hours over seven to nine months.

**Access and balance.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. Free databases, citizen science apps, documentaries and Nobel materials cover the core. You need school biology. Watch animals near you, from birds to insects, at every stage, and always respect wildlife and local rules.

# About zoology

Zoology studies animals: from sponges and insects to fish, birds and mammals. It asks how animals are built, how they work, why they behave as they do, how they evolved, and how to protect them.

This path starts with animal senses and the tools of naturalists, surveys diversity and its origins, studies physiology, behaviour and cognition, explores evolution, migration and coevolution, examines the biodiversity crisis and conservation, and ends with animal welfare, shared diseases and research.

**Trivia**
- An octopus has around 500 million neurons, about two-thirds of them in its arms.
- Arctic terns may fly over 70,000 km a year, migrating between the Arctic and Antarctic.
- The 2024 Living Planet Report found monitored wildlife populations had declined by an average of 73% since 1970.
- In 1973 Karl von Frisch, Konrad Lorenz and Niko Tinbergen shared a Nobel Prize for studying animal behaviour.

### Timeline
- **c. 350 BCE · Aristotle** writes *History of Animals*.
- **1758 · Carl Linnaeus** names animals systematically in *Systema Naturae*.
- **1859 · Charles Darwin** publishes *On the Origin of Species*.
- **1862 · Henry Walter Bates** describes mimicry in Amazonian butterflies.
- **1909 · Charles Walcott** discovers the Burgess Shale fossils.
- **1960 · Jane Goodall** begins studying chimpanzees at Gombe.
- **1963 · Niko Tinbergen** sets out four questions about behaviour.
- **1964 · The IUCN Red List** begins assessing threatened species.
- **1995 · Wolves** are reintroduced to Yellowstone.
- **2004 · *Tiktaalik*** is found, a fish with features of land animals.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will know zoology the way a strong undergraduate course expects. You will be able to:

- **Describe diversity:** the major animal groups and how they are classified.
- **Explain physiology:** how animals breathe, move, sense and keep their bodies stable.
- **Explain behaviour and cognition:** using Tinbergen's four questions and modern evidence.
- **Explain evolution in action:** adaptation, coevolution, migration and the fossil record.
- **Discuss conservation and welfare:** threats, recovery, zoonoses and how we treat animals.
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check.

These goals are a draft standard. Zoologists and biology educators from more than one country will confirm them before the path is published.

**How stages work.** Each work ends with a short quiz. Score 70% or more across a stage's quizzes to pass it. If you fall short, you can retake the quizzes, or continue anyway: the stage then stays marked **Not finished** until you come back and pass it.

# Final mastery check

Finishing all six stages makes you a capable student of zoology. To earn the **Ascent Mastery: Zoology** certificate, you also complete:

1. **The grand quiz.** 40 game-style questions across all six stages. Pass mark 80%, with unlimited retakes.
2. **Field study.** Observe a local animal species over several weeks and report its behaviour.
3. **Species profile.** Write a full profile of a threatened species, with a conservation plan.
4. **Capstone essay.** 2,500 words on one of the Stage 6 questions. Strong essays are read by an expert reviewer.
5. **Explain it.** Answer three unseen questions as if to a friend. For example: “How do birds know where to migrate?”

The certificate needs every stage passed.
