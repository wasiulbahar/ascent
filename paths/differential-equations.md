# Differential Equations

The mathematics of change: first-order equations and their models, oscillations and Laplace transforms, systems and phase portraits, nonlinear dynamics and chaos, partial differential equations, and numerical methods for real models. About 200 to 250 hours over eight to ten months.

**Access and balance.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. Free MIT lectures, free textbooks and interactive tools cover the core. You need single-variable calculus (the Calculus path) and, from Stage 3, some linear algebra. Solving equations both by hand and in code builds the deepest understanding.

# About differential equations

A differential equation relates a quantity to its rates of change. Such equations describe falling objects, cooling coffee, vibrating bridges, populations, epidemics, electric circuits, heat flow, weather and the orbits of planets.

This path begins with visual intuition, solves first-order equations and their models, studies oscillations, Laplace transforms and systems, explores nonlinear dynamics and chaos, introduces partial differential equations, and ends with numerical methods and applications in biology, climate and engineering.

**Trivia**
- Edward Lorenz discovered chaos in 1961 when rerunning a weather simulation with a number rounded from 0.506127 to 0.506 produced a completely different forecast.
- Proving that the Navier–Stokes equations of fluid flow always have smooth solutions is a $1 million Millennium Prize Problem.
- The Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapsed in 1940 from aeroelastic flutter, a self-reinforcing oscillation, not simple resonance as often claimed.
- An error in Henri Poincaré's prize-winning 1889 essay on the three-body problem led him, on correcting it, to the first glimpses of chaos.

### Timeline
- **1687 · Isaac Newton** publishes the *Principia*, with laws of motion as rates of change.
- **1739 · Leonhard Euler** solves linear equations with constant coefficients.
- **1768 · Euler** describes his step-by-step numerical method.
- **1822 · Joseph Fourier** publishes his theory of heat with Fourier series.
- **1845 · George Stokes** completes the Navier–Stokes equations of fluid flow.
- **1890 · Henri Poincaré** publishes work on the three-body problem that foreshadows chaos.
- **1901 · Runge and Kutta** complete the Runge–Kutta methods.
- **1926 · Vito Volterra** models predators and prey (also found by Lotka).
- **1927 · Kermack and McKendrick** publish the SIR epidemic model.
- **1963 · Edward Lorenz** publishes “Deterministic Nonperiodic Flow”.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will know differential equations the way a strong university course, with a taste of dynamics and PDEs, expects. You will be able to:

- **Model change:** turn situations into differential equations and interpret solutions.
- **Solve ODEs:** separable, linear, second-order and systems, including with Laplace transforms.
- **Analyse dynamics:** phase portraits, stability, bifurcations and chaos.
- **Meet PDEs:** the heat and wave equations and Fourier series.
- **Compute:** numerical solutions with Euler, Runge–Kutta and scientific software.
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check.

These goals are a draft standard. Mathematicians and applied mathematics teachers from more than one country will confirm them before the path is published.
