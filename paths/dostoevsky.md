# Dostoevsky

From a gentle love story you can finish in an evening to the critical debates that still divide scholars. About 250 to 300 hours over nine to twelve months.

**Translations and access.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. Constance Garnett's translations (1912 to 1920) are free on Project Gutenberg and fine for Stages 1 and 2. From Stage 3 on, a modern annotated translation is recommended because the notes matter at this level; these are paid, or can be borrowed from a library.

# About Dostoevsky

![Portrait of Dostoevsky by Vasily Perov: a thin, bearded man in a dark coat, hands clasped around one knee, looking down and to the side](media/dostoevsky/perov-portrait-1872.jpg)
*Vasily Perov, Portrait of Fyodor Dostoevsky, 1872. State Tretyakov Gallery, Moscow. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons. It was painted while Dostoevsky was writing Demons; his wife Anna thought it caught him in a moment of creative concentration.*

Fyodor Mikhailovich Dostoevsky (1821–1881) lived a life that reads like one of his own novels. He was sentenced to death at 28 and reprieved at the last moment. He spent four years in a Siberian prison camp, gambled away money he did not have, and wrote his greatest books against deadlines, in debt and often in exile. Out of that life came novels about murderers, saints, revolutionaries and doubters that still shape how we talk about freedom, guilt and faith. Nietzsche, Freud, Camus and many modern novelists counted him as an influence.

**Trivia**
- On 22 December 1849 he stood on a Petersburg square in front of a firing squad. A messenger arrived with the Tsar's pardon at the last minute: the whole execution had been staged. He retells the experience through Prince Myshkin in *The Idiot*.
- During his four years in the Omsk prison camp, the only book he was allowed was a New Testament, given to him on the way to Siberia by the wives of exiled Decembrists.
- He had epilepsy. He described the moments of ecstatic clarity before a seizure through Myshkin, too.
- His first published work was not a novel but a translation: Balzac's *Eugénie Grandet*, in 1844.
- His second wife, Anna, began as his stenographer and became his publisher. From 1873 the couple published his books themselves, and her business sense got them out of debt.
- Tens of thousands of people followed his coffin through St Petersburg in 1881.

### Life in dates
- **1821** Born in Moscow, son of a doctor at a hospital for the poor.
- **1839** His father dies on the family estate. Rumours that his serfs murdered him have never been proven.
- **1846** *Poor Folk* makes him famous overnight at 24.
- **1849** Arrested with the Petrashevsky Circle, a socialist discussion group. Mock execution in December.
- **1850–54** Penal servitude in Omsk, then army service in Semipalatinsk, where he marries Maria Isaeva.
- **1859** Returns to St Petersburg and starts journals with his brother Mikhail.
- **1864** Maria and Mikhail both die. *Notes from Underground*.
- **1866** *Crime and Punishment* and *The Gambler* in the same year.
- **1867–71** Marries Anna and lives abroad, mostly in Dresden, Geneva and Florence, fleeing creditors.
- **1876–81** Writes *A Writer's Diary*, his one-man magazine.
- **1880** *The Brothers Karamazov* and the Pushkin Speech.
- **1881** Dies in St Petersburg on 9 February (28 January in the old Russian calendar).

# What you will be able to do

This path is built so that, at the end, you know Dostoevsky the way a good graduate seminar would expect. You will have:

- **Read the whole major canon:** all five great novels (*Crime and Punishment*, *The Idiot*, *Demons*, *The Adolescent*, *The Brothers Karamazov*) and twelve shorter works, from his debut to the late stories.
- **Know his life** well enough to place every work in it and explain how prison, debt, epilepsy and grief shaped it.
- **Know his world:** Slavophiles and Westernizers, the radicals of the 1860s, Chernyshevsky, Russian Orthodoxy and the Petrashevsky Circle.
- **Argue the main debates** and name who holds each side: the epilogue of *Crime and Punishment*, the answer to Ivan, polyphony, politics and art.
- **Have read at least five major critics,** including one who disliked him (Nabokov).
- **Know his influence** on Nietzsche, Freud, the existentialists and twentieth-century fiction.
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check.

These goals are a draft standard. Expert reviewers will confirm them against university seminar coverage before the path is published.

# Stage 1: First Encounters
level: Beginner
intent: Short, gripping works that let you hear his voice before the big novels.
gate: Score 70% or more across this stage's quizzes.

Every great writer has a way in. For Dostoevsky it is not the thousand-page novels but three short works from three moments of his life: the hopeful young writer of 1848, the desperate gambler of 1866 and the famous prophet of the late 1870s. Together they take about eight hours, and by the end you will already recognise his signature: characters who talk to themselves in fevers of feeling, cities at night, and sudden turns from despair to grace.

## White Nights
- id: d1 · type: read · year: 1848 · length: novella, about 2 hours
- edition: Garnett, in *White Nights and Other Stories*. Any modern translation also works.
- access: free | Garnett's translation on Project Gutenberg

### Introduction
A lonely young man who calls himself the Dreamer meets a young woman crying beside a St Petersburg canal. Over four summer nights, while the sky never quite gets dark, they tell each other their lives. It is the gentlest thing Dostoevsky wrote, published a year before his arrest changed everything.

**Trivia**
- The "white nights" are real: around midsummer, St Petersburg's sun sets for only a few hours and the sky stays pale all night.
- The story has been filmed again and again, from Luchino Visconti's *Le notti bianche* (1957) to Robert Bresson and several Indian adaptations.
- Dostoevsky was 27 when it appeared. Within months he was arrested, and his career stopped for almost a decade.

### Understand
- **Context:** the "dreamer" was a stock figure of 1840s Russian writing: a young man who lives in his imagination instead of in the world. Dostoevsky is half in love with the type and half critical of it.
- **Reading guide:** four nights and a morning. The first night is meeting; the second is two life stories (his, then Nastenka's); the third and fourth are hope and reversal; the morning is the aftermath.
- **Key term:** *mechtatel'*, the dreamer.

### Analyse
- **Theme:** imagination as shelter and as prison. Is the Dreamer's final gratitude generous, or a sign that he will never live?
- **Debate:** is the story sentimental, as its subtitle "A Sentimental Novel" claims, or a gentle parody of sentimental fiction?
- **Reread:** the Dreamer's long monologue on the second night, where he describes himself in the third person.

### Scholar
- Joseph Frank, *Dostoevsky: The Seeds of Revolt, 1821–1849* (Princeton University Press, 1976), the chapters on the stories of 1847 to 1849.
- Essay question: "The Dreamer is the first of Dostoevsky's men who prefer an idea of life to life itself." Discuss.

### Quiz
**Multiple choice.** What does the hero call himself?
- A poet
- A dreamer ✓
- A copying clerk
> His whole identity is built on imagined life.

**Fill in the blank.** The story takes place over four ___ and a morning. [answer: nights]

**Multiple choice.** Why is the story called *White Nights*?
- It is set in a snowy Russian winter
- It is set in the Petersburg summer, when the sky stays light at night ✓
- The heroine always wears white
> Around midsummer the sun barely sets in St Petersburg.

## The Gambler
- id: d2 · type: read · year: 1866 · length: short novel, about 5 hours
- edition: Garnett. Modern translations are available from several publishers. [verify: name a recommended modern translator]
- access: free-and-paid | Garnett's translation is free on Project Gutenberg; modern translations are paid

### Introduction
In 1866 Dostoevsky signed a contract that said: deliver a new novel by 1 November, or the publisher Fyodor Stellovsky may print all your work for nine years without paying you. With weeks left and nothing written, he hired a young stenographer and dictated *The Gambler* in 26 days. It is a fast, funny, feverish book about roulette, debt and a love that feels like a bet, and he knew all three from the inside.

**Trivia**
- The stenographer was Anna Snitkina, aged 20. He proposed a few weeks after the deadline, and they married in February 1867.
- Dostoevsky lost money at the casinos of Wiesbaden, Baden-Baden and Homburg for years. Anna's diary records them pawning her jewellery to cover his losses.
- He finally stopped gambling in 1871 and, by Anna's account, never went back.

### Understand
- **Context:** the novel's town, "Roulettenburg", is a composite of German spa casinos. Polina is often linked to Apollinaria Suslova, with whom he had a turbulent affair.
- **Reading guide:** follow Alexei, a tutor in a Russian general's household, as everyone waits for a rich grandmother to die. The grandmother's arrival at the casino (around chapters 9 to 12) is the comic centre.
- **Key term:** the Russian character abroad, a frequent theme of Dostoevsky's journalism.

### Analyse
- **Theme:** gambling as a test of fate and of freedom. Alexei wants to defy chance; chance is indifferent to him.
- **Debate:** autobiography or self-portrait at a distance? Critics disagree on how far Alexei speaks for Dostoevsky.
- **Reread:** the grandmother's losses at roulette, and the final chapter.

### Scholar
- Joseph Frank, *Dostoevsky: The Miraculous Years, 1865–1871* (Princeton University Press, 1995), the chapters on 1866.
- Anna Dostoevsky, *Reminiscences* (her memoir of the dictation and their marriage; several English editions).
- Essay question: how does *The Gambler* turn a personal addiction into a question about freedom?

### Quiz
**Fill in the blank.** Dostoevsky dictated *The Gambler* in ___ days. [answer: 26]
> He had to beat a contract deadline of 1 November 1866.

**Multiple choice.** What would have happened if he had missed the deadline?
- He would have gone to a debtors' prison
- His publisher could print his work for nine years without paying him ✓
- He would have lost his army pension
> The publisher was Fyodor Stellovsky.

**Multiple choice.** Who took down the dictation?
- His brother Mikhail
- Apollinaria Suslova
- Anna Snitkina, who later became his wife ✓

## The Dream of a Ridiculous Man
- id: d3 · type: read · year: 1877 · length: short story, about 1 hour
- edition: Garnett. Also in most modern collections of his short fiction.
- access: free | Garnett's translation on Project Gutenberg

### Introduction
A man who has decided that nothing matters plans to shoot himself tonight. On the way home he ignores a little girl begging for help, and that small cruelty bothers him more than it should. He falls asleep and dreams of an earth where no one has ever sinned. Twenty pages hold most of Dostoevsky's big questions: why evil starts, and whether love is still possible once you know it.

**Trivia**
- It first appeared in *A Writer's Diary* in 1877, between pieces of journalism about court cases and politics.
- Dostoevsky subtitled it "A Fantastic Story", the label he used for tales that bend reality.
- Many readers treat it as the shortest route into *The Brothers Karamazov*, which he began soon after.

### Understand
- **Context:** *A Writer's Diary* was the one-man magazine Dostoevsky wrote in the 1870s. This story sits beside his journalism, not apart from it.
- **Reading guide:** five short chapters: the decision, the girl, the dream journey, the corruption of paradise, the awakening.
- **Key term:** utopia, and the fall.

### Analyse
- **Theme:** the narrator corrupts the innocent world himself. Is evil learned, or carried in?
- **Debate:** is this a Christian parable or a story about the limits of any utopia, religious or political?
- **Reread:** how the paradise falls, step by step, starting with a single lie.

### Scholar
- Rowan Williams, *Dostoevsky: Language, Faith and Fiction* (Baylor University Press, 2008).
- Essay question: why does the narrator need to corrupt paradise before he can love the real world?

### Quiz
**Multiple choice.** What does the narrator plan to do at the start?
- Leave Russia for good
- Kill himself ✓
- Confess to a crime

**Multiple choice.** What happens to the sinless world in his dream?
- He becomes its king
- He corrupts it ✓
- He is driven out of it

**Fill in the blank.** The corruption of paradise begins with a single ___. [answer: lie]

# Stage 2: The Great Entry Novel
level: Explorer
intent: One complete masterpiece, read slowly, with company.
gate: Score 70% or more across this stage's quizzes.

*Crime and Punishment* is where most people fall for Dostoevsky, and for good reason. It moves like a thriller, yet it is a novel about ideas: what happens to a person who decides that morality is a rule for ordinary people only. Give it three or four weeks. The reading guide below breaks it into six parts, so it never feels like a wall.

## Crime and Punishment
- id: d4 · type: read · year: 1866 · length: novel, about 25 hours
- edition: Oliver Ready (Penguin, 2014) for readability, or Richard Pevear and Larissa Volokhonsky (1992) for closeness to the Russian.
- access: free-and-paid | Garnett's translation is free on Project Gutenberg; Ready and Pevear-Volokhonsky are paid

### Introduction
A poor former student believes some people are extraordinary enough to step over the moral law. He decides to test it on himself and kills a pawnbroker with an axe. Everything after the murder is the punishment, long before any court: fever, fear, a detective who never hurries, and a young woman, Sonya, who offers him a way back.

**Trivia**
- Dostoevsky first planned it as a short first-person confession, then burned that draft and started again in the third person.
- He was writing it and *The Gambler* in the same autumn of 1866, dictating one while still serialising the other. [verify: his daily working pattern that autumn]
- The hero's surname comes from *raskol*, Russian for "schism" or "split".
- The novel says it is exactly 730 steps from Raskolnikov's gate to the pawnbroker's building. Visitors to St Petersburg still walk the route.

### Understand
- **Context:** serialised in *The Russian Messenger* through 1866. The 1860s were full of radical theories that reason and self-interest could rebuild morality. Raskolnikov's theory is a nightmare version of them.
- **Reading guide:** six parts and an epilogue. Part 1 is the murder. Parts 2 to 4 are fever, family and Sonya. Parts 5 and 6 tighten into confession. The epilogue moves to Siberia.
- **Key terms:** the "extraordinary man" theory; *raskol*.

### Analyse
- **Themes:** the split self; suffering as a path to renewal; the city as a stifling body.
- **Debate 1, the motive.** Raskolnikov gives several reasons: money for his family, to prove he is a Napoleon, simply to find out whether he can. Critics disagree on which one the novel treats as true, or whether the point is that none is.
- **Debate 2, the epilogue.** Is Raskolnikov's turn toward renewal in Siberia earned by the novel, or imposed on it? Many critics find it rushed; others argue the whole book prepares it.
- **Reread:** Raskolnikov's article "On Crime" as Porfiry describes it (Part 3, chapter 5); Sonya reading the raising of Lazarus (Part 4, chapter 4).

### Scholar
- Joseph Frank, *Dostoevsky: The Miraculous Years, 1865–1871* (Princeton University Press, 1995), the chapters on *Crime and Punishment*.
- Joseph Frank, *Dostoevsky: A Writer in His Time*, one-volume edition, ed. Mary Petrusewicz (Princeton University Press, 2009) [verify: year of the one-volume edition], the same chapters in shorter form.
- Essay question: is the epilogue the novel's weakest part, or its key?

### Quiz
**Multiple choice.** Who is the investigator who slowly closes in on Raskolnikov?
- Svidrigailov
- Porfiry Petrovich ✓
- Razumikhin

**Fill in the blank.** Raskolnikov's name comes from *raskol*, a Russian word meaning "___". [answer: schism / split]

- That the theory was right: he feels free
- That the theory collapses: he is sick, terrified and cannot even use the money ✓
- That he has no conscience at all
> The novel tests the idea against a body.

**Multiple choice.** Why does Sonya read him the story of Lazarus?
- It is a story of someone raised from the dead, which frames his possible renewal ✓
- It is her favourite story from childhood
- She wants to frighten him into confessing

# Stage 3: Range
level: Reader
intent: Three very different books, from the philosophical to the political.
gate: Score 70% or more across this stage's quizzes.

Now you know his voice, it's time to see how far it reaches. This stage covers a furious philosophical monologue, a novel about a truly good man, and a political thriller based on a real murder. Each looks like a different writer at first; by the end of the stage you will see the one mind behind all three. You will also read his prison memoir, the experience underneath everything else.

## Notes from Underground
- id: d6 · type: read · year: 1864 · length: about 5 hours
- edition: Pevear and Volokhonsky (1993), or Michael R. Katz (Norton Critical Edition), whose notes and essays are valuable here.
- access: free-and-paid | Garnett's translation is free on Project Gutenberg; annotated editions are paid

### Introduction
"I am a sick man." A retired clerk, alone in his room, argues with everyone who believes people act in their own interest. He insists humans will do something self-destructive simply to prove they are free. It is short, bitter and often very funny, and it is the book most often called the start of existentialism.

**Trivia**
- Nietzsche came across a French translation in a bookshop in 1887 and wrote to a friend that instinct had told him at once he had found a kindred spirit.
- The "Crystal Palace" he mocks was real: the glass hall of London's Great Exhibition of 1851, which Dostoevsky visited in 1862.
- The narrator never gives his name. Readers simply call him the Underground Man.

### Understand
- **Context:** a polemic against Nikolai Chernyshevsky's novel *What Is to Be Done?* (1863) and its "rational egoism". The Crystal Palace stands for a perfectly rational future society.
- **Reading guide:** Part One, "Underground", is a monologue of ideas. Part Two, "Apropos of the Wet Snow", shows the same man twenty years earlier, failing in life.
- **Key terms:** rational egoism; "two times two makes four"; ressentiment (a later term often applied to him).
- **Censorship:** Dostoevsky complained in a letter to his brother Mikhail (March 1864) that the censors had cut the passage in which he drew the need for faith in Christ out of the argument.

### Analyse
- **Debate:** mouthpiece or satire? Some read the Underground Man as Dostoevsky's own critique of reason; others as a portrait of where that critique ends up, a man paralysed by his own freedom.
- **Reread:** the Crystal Palace passages (Part One, chapters 7 to 10); the scene with Liza at the end of Part Two.

### Scholar
- Joseph Frank, *Dostoevsky: The Stir of Liberation, 1860–1865* (Princeton University Press, 1986), on Chernyshevsky and the *Notes*.
- Essay question: does Part Two refute Part One, or prove it?

### Quiz
**Fill in the blank.** The book opens: "I am a ___ man." [answer: sick]

**Multiple choice.** Which novel is *Notes from Underground* arguing against?
- Turgenev's *Fathers and Sons*
- Chernyshevsky's *What Is to Be Done?* ✓
- Goncharov's *Oblomov*

**Multiple choice.** What does the Crystal Palace stand for in the book?
- The glamour of Western Europe
- A perfectly rational future society, where people act only in their own interest ✓
- The Tsar's power

**Multiple choice.** Critics disagree about the Underground Man. What is the main question?
- Whether he is Dostoevsky's mouthpiece or a satire of where his ideas lead ✓
- Whether he is based on Gogol
- Whether he is the same man as Raskolnikov

## The Idiot
- id: d7 · type: read · year: 1868–69 · length: about 25 hours
- edition: Pevear and Volokhonsky (2001).
- access: free-and-paid | Garnett's translation is free on Project Gutenberg; Pevear-Volokhonsky is paid

### Introduction
Dostoevsky set himself the hardest task he could imagine: to portray a "positively beautiful" man. Prince Myshkin returns to Russia from a Swiss clinic, honest, kind and unable to lie. Society calls him an idiot, two women love him, and a jealous merchant shadows him. The novel asks whether pure goodness can survive among people, and its ending is one of the most shocking in Russian literature.

![Holbein's painting of the dead Christ: a long, narrow panel showing a gaunt body lying in a tomb](media/dostoevsky/holbein-dead-christ.jpg)
*Hans Holbein the Younger, The Body of the Dead Christ in the Tomb, 1521–22. Kunstmuseum Basel. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.*

**Trivia**
- In Basel in 1867, Dostoevsky stood so long and so close in front of Holbein's painting *The Body of the Dead Christ in the Tomb* that Anna feared it would bring on a seizure. A copy of that painting hangs in the novel.
- Myshkin's story of a man led out to be shot is Dostoevsky's own mock execution, told almost twenty years later.
- He wrote it in poverty abroad, and the couple's first child, Sonya, was born and died during its writing.

### Understand
- **Context:** the "positively beautiful man" phrase comes from his letter to his niece Sofia Ivanova of January 1868.
- **Painting:** Hans Holbein the Younger, *The Body of the Dead Christ in the Tomb* (1521–22), Kunstmuseum Basel. Myshkin says such a painting could make a man lose his faith.

### Analyse
- **Debate:** Christ figure or failure? Does Myshkin's goodness heal anyone, or does his passivity help cause the catastrophe?
- **Reread:** the Holbein conversation (Part 2, chapter 4); Ippolit's "Necessary Explanation" (Part 3, chapters 5 to 7).

### Scholar
- Joseph Frank, *The Miraculous Years* (1995), the chapters on *The Idiot*.
- Essay question: is Myshkin's goodness a gift to the other characters or a danger to them?

### Quiz
**Multiple choice.** What did Dostoevsky set out to portray in Prince Myshkin?
- A holy fool who sees the future
- A "positively beautiful" man ✓
- A self-portrait of his younger self

**Fill in the blank.** Myshkin says Holbein's painting of the dead Christ could make a man lose his ___. [answer: faith]

**Multiple choice.** Which of Dostoevsky's own experiences does Myshkin retell?
- His years in the Omsk prison camp
- His mock execution in 1849 ✓
- His gambling at Baden-Baden

## Demons
- id: d8 · type: read · year: 1871–72 · length: about 30 hours
- edition: Pevear and Volokhonsky (1994), or Michael R. Katz (Oxford World's Classics). Older translations call it *The Possessed* or *The Devils*.
- access: free-and-paid | Garnett's translation, as *The Possessed*, is free on Project Gutenberg; modern translations are paid

### Introduction
In 1869 a revolutionary cell led by Sergei Nechayev murdered one of its own members, a student named Ivan Ivanov, in Moscow. Dostoevsky turned the case into a dark comedy that becomes a tragedy: a quiet provincial town where fashionable ideas possess people one by one until they end in murder. Twentieth-century readers saw it as a prophecy of revolutionary terror.

**Trivia**
- The title comes from a Pushkin poem and from the Gospel story of demons driven into a herd of pigs, which rush into a lake.
- His editor refused to print the chapter in which the hero, Stavrogin, confesses a terrible crime. It stayed unpublished until 1922.
- Albert Camus adapted the novel for the stage in Paris in 1959.

### Understand
- **Context:** the Nechayev affair; the gap between the liberal "men of the forties" (Stepan Trofimovich) and their radical sons (Pyotr Verkhovensky).
- **Epigraphs:** Pushkin's poem "Demons" and Luke 8, the Gadarene swine.
- **Suppressed chapter:** "At Tikhon's", refused by the editor Mikhail Katkov. Modern editions print it as an appendix.

### Analyse
- **Debate:** prophecy or caricature? Is the novel a profound diagnosis of political nihilism, or a reactionary satire unfair to the radicals of its time?
- **Characters to track:** Stavrogin (charisma with nothing inside), Kirillov (suicide as proof of freedom), Shatov (faith he wants but cannot reach).

### Scholar
- Joseph Frank, *The Miraculous Years* (1995), the chapters on *Demons*.
- Essay question: which of the "possessed" does the novel treat with the most sympathy, and why?

### Quiz
**Multiple choice.** Which real event inspired *Demons*?
- The assassination of Tsar Alexander II
- The murder of a student by Sergei Nechayev's revolutionary cell ✓
- The Decembrist revolt

**Fill in the blank.** The chapter his editor refused to print is called "At ___'s". [answer: Tikhon]

**Multiple choice.** What does Kirillov believe his suicide will prove?
- That God exists
- That he is completely free ✓
- That the revolution has failed

## Notes from the House of the Dead
- id: d9 · type: read · year: 1860–62 · length: about 12 hours
- edition: Garnett, or a modern translation.
- access: free-and-paid | Garnett's translation, as *The House of the Dead*, is free on Project Gutenberg; modern translations are paid

### Introduction
A semi-fictional account of the four years Dostoevsky spent in the prison camp at Omsk, told through a narrator convicted of murdering his wife. It is a portrait of murderers, thieves and ordinary peasants that changed how Russians thought about prison, and it is where his faith in the Russian people began.

**Trivia**
- Tolstoy called it one of the best books in Russian literature. [verify: source of Tolstoy's remark]

# Stage 4: Summit
level: Connoisseur
intent: His final novel, read as the culmination of everything before it.
gate: Score 70% or more across this stage's quizzes.

*The Brothers Karamazov* is the book Dostoevsky was building toward all his life, and many readers call it the greatest novel ever written. Everything you have met so far returns here: the murderer's theory, the good man, the rebel, the believer, the court case. Give it two months. You will finish it able to read anything.

## The Brothers Karamazov
- id: d10 · type: read · year: 1879–80 · length: about 40 hours
- edition: Pevear and Volokhonsky (1990), or Ignat Avsey (Oxford World's Classics).
- access: free-and-paid | Garnett's translation is free on Project Gutenberg; modern translations are paid

### Introduction
A dissolute father, three very different sons and a fourth who may also be his son. Then the father is murdered. One brother believes in God, one rebels against him, one lives by passion, and the novel lets each make his strongest case. Inside it is "The Grand Inquisitor", a story told by one brother to another that philosophers still teach on its own.

**Trivia**
- The youngest brother is named Alyosha after Dostoevsky's own three-year-old son, who died of epilepsy in 1878 while the novel was being planned.
- After that loss he visited the Optina Monastery with the young philosopher Vladimir Solovyov. The elder Zosima draws on what he saw there.
- He meant it as the first half of a longer story. He died about three months after finishing it.
- Freud called it the most magnificent novel ever written. [verify: wording in "Dostoevsky and Parricide"]

### Understand
- **Context:** the novel ends a decade of Dostoevsky's journalism about Russia, faith and justice, and draws on real court cases he followed.
- **Reading guide:** twelve books and an epilogue. Book V, "Pro and Contra", holds "Rebellion" and "The Grand Inquisitor". Book VI, "The Russian Monk", is Zosima's life and teachings. Books VII to XII are the murder, the investigation and the trial.
- **Epigraph:** John 12:24, the grain of wheat that must die to bear fruit.

### Analyse
- **Central debate, the answer to Ivan.** Ivan's "Rebellion" refuses any harmony bought with the suffering of children. In a letter to Konstantin Pobedonostsev (August 1879), Dostoevsky called Book VI his answer to that negative side, and said he was anxious whether it would be sufficient. Does the novel answer Ivan with an argument, with a life, or not at all?
- **Debate 2, the author's voice.** Mikhail Bakhtin argued the novel lets opposing voices stand unresolved; others read it as a Christian novel that quietly decides the argument. This leads into Stage 6.
- **Reread:** Book V, chapters 4 and 5; Book VI, chapters 2 and 3; Alyosha's speech at the stone in the Epilogue.

- Sigmund Freud, "Dostoevsky and Parricide" (1928).

# Stage 5: The Whole Writer
level: Specialist
intent: Complete the canon: his debut, the fifth great novel, and the late short masterpieces.
gate: Score 70% or more across this stage's quizzes.

Most readers stop after *Karamazov*. Specialists don't. This stage fills in the rest: the debut that made him famous, the strange early novella about a double, the least-read of his five great novels, and the short works he wrote in the margins of his journalism. Reading them in light of everything else is where you start to see the shape of a whole career.
