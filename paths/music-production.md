# Music Production

How recorded music is made: the physics of sound and recording, working in a digital audio workstation, synthesis, MIDI and sampling, mixing and mastering, the history of studio innovation, and rights, streaming and protecting your hearing. About 150 to 200 hours over six to eight months, much of it making music.

**Access and balance.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. Free software (BandLab, Audacity, LMMS), interactive lessons and magazine archives cover the core; headphones and a computer or phone are enough to start. The Music theory path helps but isn't required. The path covers production traditions from many genres and countries.

# About music production

Music production is the craft of turning musical ideas into finished recordings: capturing sound, arranging, programming, editing, mixing and mastering. Today a whole studio can fit on a laptop or phone.

This path starts with the story of recording and how hit songs are made, teaches sound, microphones and recording, works in a DAW with synthesis, MIDI and sampling, covers mixing and mastering, studies the inventors and genres that changed production, and ends with rights, streaming, hearing health and releasing your music.

**Trivia**
- The first words Thomas Edison recorded on his phonograph in 1877 were “Mary had a little lamb”.
- Delia Derbyshire realised the original *Doctor Who* theme in 1963 by cutting and splicing tape, without synthesisers.
- The MIDI standard, agreed in 1983, still lets electronic instruments and computers from different makers talk to each other.
- Les Paul pioneered multitrack recording, layering his own guitar and voice in the late 1940s and 1950s.

### Timeline
- **1877 · Thomas Edison** records sound on the phonograph.
- **1948 · Magnetic tape recorders** transform studio recording.
- **1950s · Les Paul** popularises multitrack recording.
- **1958 · The BBC Radiophonic Workshop** is founded.
- **1964 · Robert Moog** introduces his modular synthesiser.
- **1970s · Jamaican producers such as King Tubby** invent dub, using the mixing desk as an instrument.
- **1983 · MIDI** becomes a standard.
- **2001 · Ableton Live** is released.
- **2010 · EBU R 128** sets loudness standards for broadcast.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will produce music the way a capable independent producer does. You will be able to:

- **Understand sound:** frequency, amplitude, timbre and room acoustics.
- **Record well:** microphones, gain staging and clean takes.
- **Produce in a DAW:** arranging, MIDI, synthesis and sampling.
- **Mix and master:** EQ, compression, space, balance and loudness standards.
- **Work professionally:** rights, royalties, streaming, release and hearing protection.
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check.

These goals are a draft standard. Producers, engineers and music educators from more than one country will confirm them before the path is published.

**How stages work.** Each work ends with a short quiz. Score 70% or more across a stage's quizzes to pass it. If you fall short, you can retake the quizzes, or continue anyway: the stage then stays marked **Not finished** until you come back and pass it.

# Final mastery check

Finishing all six stages makes you a capable music producer. To earn the **Ascent Mastery: Music Production** certificate, you also complete:

1. **The grand quiz.** 40 game-style questions across all six stages. Pass mark 80%, with unlimited retakes.
2. **EP project.** Produce three finished tracks, recorded, mixed and mastered.
3. **Production notes.** Document your process for one track, from idea to master.
4. **Capstone essay.** 2,500 words on one of the Stage 6 questions. Strong essays are read by an expert reviewer.
5. **Explain it.** Answer three unseen questions as if to a new producer. For example: “Why does my mix sound muddy?”

The certificate needs every stage passed.
