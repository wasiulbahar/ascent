# Biology

From the wonder of living things to the molecules, genes and ecosystems behind them. About 300 to 360 hours over twelve to fifteen months.

**Access and balance.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. Excellent free material exists, including a complete university textbook and Darwin's own works. The path tells biology as a world story, and it faces the field's ethical questions directly. From Stage 2 some quizzes include short problems; you can type the number alone.

# About biology

Biology is the science of life: how living things are built, how they work, how they inherit their traits, how they interact, and how they came to be. One idea ties it all together, evolution by natural selection. As the geneticist Theodosius Dobzhansky put it, nothing in biology makes sense except in the light of evolution.

This path begins with great storytellers of life, builds the basics with free courses and Darwin himself, then covers genes, cells, evolution and ecology, reaches the core of a university biology degree, and ends with the ethics and debates of modern biology.

**Trivia**
- Every cell in your body (apart from red blood cells) carries about two metres of DNA, folded into a nucleus far too small to see.
- Gregor Mendel worked out the laws of inheritance by breeding pea plants in a monastery garden, and published them in 1866. Almost nobody noticed until 1900.
- In the early 1900s the Indian scientist Jagadish Chandra Bose built instruments sensitive enough to record the tiny growth and electrical responses of plants.
- The mitochondria in your cells were once free-living bacteria, swallowed by another cell about two billion years ago.
- Rosalind Franklin's X-ray image of DNA, "Photo 51", was key to discovering the double helix, but she was never awarded a Nobel prize.

### Timeline
- **1665 · Cells** Robert Hooke sees and names cells under a microscope.
- **1859 · Darwin** *On the Origin of Species* sets out evolution by natural selection.
- **1866 · Mendel** The laws of inheritance, from pea plants.
- **1977 · Reading DNA** Frederick Sanger and others develop methods to read the sequence of DNA.
- **1928 · Penicillin** Alexander Fleming discovers the first antibiotic.
- **1953 · DNA** The double helix, from the work of Watson, Crick, Franklin and Wilkins.
- **1962 · Silent Spring** Rachel Carson starts the modern environmental movement.
- **2003 · The human genome** The Human Genome Project completes its sequence.
- **2012 · CRISPR** Doudna and Charpentier show a precise way to edit genes.

# Before you start

You need only school science to begin. The path adds the rest:

- **Stages 1 and 2:** no prerequisites; Stage 2 builds the basics of cells, molecules and genes.
- **Stage 3:** basic chemistry (atoms, bonds, molecules), covered in the free OpenStax textbook in Stage 2.
- **Stages 4 and 5:** simple statistics and percentages for genetics and ecology. Khan Academy's free statistics units fill any gaps.

Already know some biology? Take the **placement test** on this path's page to skip the stages you have mastered.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will know biology the way a strong first and second year of a university biology degree expects. You will be able to:

- **Explain evolution by natural selection,** and the evidence for it from fossils, genes and living populations.
- **Describe how cells work:** DNA, RNA and proteins, energy, and cell division.
- **Solve basic problems** in genetics and population biology, such as Mendelian crosses and Hardy–Weinberg.
- **Think ecologically:** food webs, energy flow and biodiversity.
- **Weigh the debates and ethics:** gene-centred versus systems views of life, and consent in medical research.
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check.

These goals are a draft standard. Biologists from more than one field will confirm them before the path is published.

**How stages work.** Each work ends with a short quiz. Score 70% or more across a stage's quizzes to pass it. If you fall short, you can retake the quizzes, or continue anyway: the stage then stays marked **Not finished** until you come back and pass it.

In 1951 cells were taken from a poor Black woman's cancer, without her knowledge, at Johns Hopkins Hospital. They became HeLa, the first human cells to keep growing in a laboratory, used in vaccines, cancer research and thousands of studies. Her family knew nothing for decades. Skloot tells the science and the human story, and raises the questions of consent and justice that biology still faces.
