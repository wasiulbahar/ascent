# Geopolitics

From maps that explain the news to the theories that divide foreign-policy experts. About 200 to 250 hours over nine to twelve months.

**Access and balance.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**; many classic texts are free because they are old enough to be public domain. Geopolitics deals with live conflicts and rival national interests. Every school of thought is presented in its strongest form, with voices from Europe, the Americas, Russia, China, India, the Middle East and Africa. Ascent takes no side, and the case studies are reviewed regularly as events change.

# About geopolitics

Geopolitics is the study of how geography shapes power: how mountains, rivers, seas, resources and distance push states toward some choices and away from others. Why has Russia always feared invasion across the flat North European Plain? Why does so much of the world's oil pass through one narrow strait? Why do China and India watch each other across the Himalayas? Geopolitics starts with the map and asks what it makes likely.

It is also a field with a troubled history and fierce arguments. Its early thinkers advised empires, and one version of it was used to justify Nazi expansion. Today, scholars argue over how much geography really decides, compared with ideas, economics, technology and the choices of leaders. This path teaches you to read the map, then to read the people who read the map, and finally to make up your own mind.

**Trivia**
- The word "geopolitics" was coined in 1899 by the Swedish political scientist Rudolf Kjellén.
- Halford Mackinder, who gave the most famous lecture in the field's history in 1904, was also the first person to climb Mount Kenya, in 1899.
- About a fifth of the oil the world uses passes through the Strait of Hormuz, between Iran and Oman, which is about 33 km wide at its narrowest point, according to the US Energy Information Administration.
- In March 2021 a single container ship, the *Ever Given*, ran aground in the Suez Canal and blocked it for six days, holding up hundreds of ships.
- After 1945 the word "geopolitics" was so tainted by its use in Nazi Germany that many Western scholars avoided it for decades.

### Timeline of ideas
- **c. 400 BCE · Thucydides** The history of the Peloponnesian War: power, fear and honour as causes of war.
- **c. 300 BCE · Kautilya** The *Arthashastra*, an Indian manual of statecraft: your neighbour is your natural rival, your neighbour's neighbour your natural friend.
- **1377 · Ibn Khaldun** The *Muqaddimah*: why dynasties rise on group solidarity and decline within a few generations.
- **1890 · Mahan** *The Influence of Sea Power upon History*: navies and trade routes decide who leads.
- **1904 · Mackinder** "The Geographical Pivot of History": the land power that controls the heart of Eurasia has the advantage.
- **1920s–40s · German Geopolitik** Karl Haushofer's ideas of "living space", later used by the Nazis. The field's dark chapter.
- **1944 · Spykman** *The Geography of the Peace*: the coastal "rimland" around Eurasia matters more than its heartland.
- **1947 · Kennan** The "X" article sets out containment of the Soviet Union, the strategy of the Cold War.
- **1948–79 · Realism** Morgenthau, then Waltz: states seek power and security in a world with no government above them.
- **1989–96 · After the Cold War** Fukuyama's "End of History" and Huntington's "Clash of Civilizations" offer rival forecasts.
- **1990s–today · Critical and non-Western geopolitics** Scholars ask whose maps these are, and voices from Asia, Africa and Latin America challenge a Western-centred field.
- **2010s–today · Geoeconomics and technology** Energy, semiconductors, sanctions, undersea cables and climate become the new terrain of rivalry.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will know geopolitics the way a good university international-relations sequence expects. You will be able to:

- **Read a map strategically:** explain how terrain, chokepoints, borders, resources and climate shape the choices of at least ten major states and regions.
- **Explain the main schools of thought** in their strongest form, and name who holds each: classical geopolitics, realism (classical, structural, offensive), liberalism, constructivism, critical geopolitics, and non-Western traditions from India, China, the Islamic world and the Global South.
- **Know the founding texts,** many of them in the original: Thucydides, Kautilya, Ibn Khaldun, Mahan, Mackinder, Spykman, Kennan.
- **Analyse a live case** (such as the war in Ukraine, the South China Sea, or water in South Asia) through at least three lenses, and say where they disagree.
- **Use real data:** military spending, trade and energy flows from free public databases.
- **Have read at least five major scholars,** including critics of mainstream geopolitics.
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges, including a full case analysis, and passed the final mastery check.

You don't need any theory to start. This stage begins with a bestselling book of ten maps, a 2,400-year-old dialogue that still describes power politics better than most newspapers, a sweeping history of the routes that connected the world, and a documentary in which a former US defence secretary looks back on the wars he helped run. By the end, you'll look at a map and see pressures, not just borders.

## Prisoners of Geography
- id: g1 · type: read · creator: Tim Marshall · year: 2015 · length: about 8 hours
- access: paid | buy, or borrow from a library

### Introduction
Ten maps, ten regions, and a simple argument: leaders are constrained by mountains, rivers, seas and concrete. Marshall, a former foreign correspondent, explains why Russia keeps pushing west, why China cares so much about Tibet and Taiwan, and why the United States is so hard to attack. It is the ideal first book, and later stages will show you its limits.

**Trivia**
- Each chapter is built around a single map: Russia, China, the United States, Western Europe, Africa, the Middle East, India and Pakistan, Korea and Japan, Latin America and the Arctic.
- Marshall spent years as a diplomatic editor reporting from conflicts, including in the Balkans and the Middle East.
- It became an international bestseller and inspired a series of follow-up books.

### Understand
- **Key idea:** geography does not determine everything, but it sets the menu of options.
- **Reading guide:** read the Russia chapter first, then any region you care about, then the rest.
- **Key terms:** buffer zone; chokepoint; warm-water port; strategic depth.

### Analyse
- **Debate:** geographical determinism. Critics argue books like this make geography sound like destiny and leave out ideas, economics and choice. Keep a list of places where the book's explanation feels too simple.

## The Melian Dialogue
- id: g2 · type: read · creator: Thucydides, *History of the Peloponnesian War*, Book 5 · year: about 400 BCE · length: about 30 minutes
- edition: Richard Crawley's translation (1874).
- access: free | Crawley's translation is on Project Gutenberg

### Introduction
In 416 BCE the mighty Athenian empire demands that the small island of Melos submit. The Melians appeal to justice, hope and the gods; the Athenians reply with pure power. It is the most famous passage in the history of international politics, and it takes half an hour to read.

**Trivia**
- The Athenians' line "the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must" is quoted in almost every introduction to international relations.
- Melos refused to surrender. Athens besieged it, killed its men and enslaved its women and children.
- Thucydides fought in the war himself as an Athenian general, and was exiled after failing to save a city from capture.

### Analyse
- **Debate:** is the dialogue an endorsement of power politics, or a warning about the arrogance that later led Athens to disaster in Sicily? Realists and their critics read it in opposite ways.

- Athens besieged it, killed its men and enslaved its women and children ✓
- Sparta rescued it

**Multiple choice.** Who wrote the *History of the Peloponnesian War*?
- Herodotus

## The Geographical Pivot of History
- id: g7 · type: read · creator: Halford Mackinder · year: 1904 · length: about 1 hour
- access: free | public domain; widely available online

### Introduction
A lecture to the Royal Geographical Society in London that became the most famous text in classical geopolitics. Mackinder argued that the vast interior of Eurasia, out of reach of sea power, was the "pivot" of world politics, and that railways would let a land power exploit it.

**Trivia**
- He later summed it up in a famous line (1919): "Who rules East Europe commands the Heartland."
- He was also director of the London School of Economics and a member of Parliament.

### Analyse
- **Debate:** Mackinder (land power) against Mahan (sea power). Who has history proved right? Most scholars say neither, entirely.

# Stage 3: Schools of Thought
level: Reader
intent: The great theories of international politics, each in its strongest form.

Geopolitics sits inside a bigger field, international relations, which is divided into schools that explain the same events in completely different ways. This stage gives you each school's founding text, and its sharpest critic. By the end you'll be able to take any news story and say how a realist, a liberal and a constructivist would read it.

## Politics Among Nations (chapter 1)
- id: g11 · type: read · creator: Hans J. Morgenthau · year: 1948 · length: about 2 hours for chapter 1
- access: paid | buy, or borrow from a library

### Introduction
The founding text of classical realism. Morgenthau, a refugee from Nazi Germany, sets out "six principles": politics is governed by objective laws rooted in human nature, and states pursue their interests defined as power.

**Trivia**

Most introductions to geopolitics are written from London, Washington or Berlin. This stage corrects that. You'll read an ancient Indian manual of statecraft, a medieval North African theory of rising and falling states, and contemporary thinkers from China, Singapore, India and beyond who see the world order very differently.

## Arthashastra (selections)
- id: g18 · type: read · creator: Kautilya · year: roughly 300 BCE to 300 CE · length: about 5 hours for selections
- edition: R. Shamasastry's translation (1915) is free; L. N. Rangarajan's (Penguin, 1992) is easier to read.
- access: free-and-paid | Shamasastry's 1915 translation is public domain and free online; Rangarajan is paid

### Introduction
An ancient Indian treatise on statecraft, traditionally attributed to Kautilya, adviser to the emperor Chandragupta Maurya. Its "mandala" theory pictures kingdoms in circles: your neighbour is your natural rival, and your neighbour's neighbour your natural friend.

**Trivia**
- The text was lost for centuries until a manuscript was found in 1905 and translated by R. Shamasastry.
- Kautilya is often compared to Machiavelli, though the *Arthashastra* is more than a thousand years older.

## The Muqaddimah (selections)
- id: g19 · type: read · creator: Ibn Khaldun · year: 1377 · length: about 6 hours for selections
- edition: Franz Rosenthal's translation, abridged and edited by N. J. Dawood (Princeton).
- access: paid | buy, or borrow from a library

### Introduction
A North African scholar's introduction to world history, and one of the first attempts at a science of society. Ibn Khaldun argues that dynasties rise on *asabiyya*, a tribe's group solidarity, then grow soft in luxury and fall within a few generations.

**Trivia**
- He wrote it in about five months while living in a remote fortress in present-day Algeria.
