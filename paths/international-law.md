# International Law

The rules between states and beyond: where international law comes from, treaties and courts, the use of force, war crimes and genocide, the sea, climate, trade and space, and critical views from the Global South. About 180 to 220 hours over seven to nine months.

**Access and balance.** Every title is labelled **Free**, **Free + paid** or **Paid**. Treaties, court judgments, UN lectures and legal blogs are free, so primary sources are at the heart of this path. No legal training is needed. The path presents international law's achievements and its failures, and voices from many regions. It is for learning, not legal advice.

# About international law

International law governs relations between states and, increasingly, international organisations, companies and individuals. It covers war and peace, human rights, the oceans, trade, the environment and outer space.

This path starts with how people came to believe states could be bound by law, studies sources, treaties and courts, examines the rules on force and the UN Security Council, covers genocide, war crimes and international criminal justice, explores the sea, climate, trade and space, and ends with critiques and research.

**Trivia**
- Hugo Grotius, a founder of international law, escaped from prison in 1621 hidden in a chest of books.
- Raphael Lemkin coined the word “genocide” in 1944 and campaigned for the 1948 Genocide Convention.
- The International Court of Justice in The Hague has 15 judges, elected by the UN General Assembly and Security Council.
- In 2025 the International Court of Justice gave a unanimous advisory opinion that states have legal obligations to protect the climate.

### Timeline
- **1625 · Hugo Grotius** publishes *On the Law of War and Peace*.
- **1648 · The Peace of Westphalia** is often seen as the start of the modern state system.
- **1864 · The first Geneva Convention** protects wounded soldiers.
- **1928 · The Kellogg–Briand Pact** renounces war as a tool of national policy.
- **1945 · The UN Charter** bans the use of force; the Nuremberg trials begin.
- **1948 · The Genocide Convention** is adopted.
- **1969 · The Vienna Convention** codifies the law of treaties.
- **1982 · The UN Convention on the Law of the Sea** is adopted.
- **2002 · The International Criminal Court** begins work.
- **2025 · The ICJ** gives its advisory opinion on climate change.

# What you will be able to do

At the end of this path you will understand international law the way a strong university course expects. You will be able to:

- **Identify sources:** treaties, custom, general principles and how courts use them.
- **Explain institutions:** the UN, the International Court of Justice and international criminal courts.
- **Analyse the use of force:** the ban, self-defence, the Security Council and the responsibility to protect.
- **Explain international criminal and humanitarian law:** genocide, war crimes and the Geneva Conventions.
- **Apply it to global issues:** the sea, climate, trade and space, and weigh critiques.
- **For the Mastery certificate:** have written the Scholar challenges and passed the final mastery check.

These goals are a draft standard. International lawyers and law teachers from more than one region will confirm them before the path is published.

**How stages work.** Each work ends with a short quiz. Score 70% or more across a stage's quizzes to pass it. If you fall short, you can retake the quizzes, or continue anyway: the stage then stays marked **Not finished** until you come back and pass it.

# Final mastery check

Finishing all six stages makes you a capable student of international law. To earn the **Ascent Mastery: International Law** certificate, you also complete:

1. **The grand quiz.** 40 game-style questions across all six stages. Pass mark 80%, with unlimited retakes.
2. **Case analysis.** Analyse a real ICJ or ICC case: facts, law, reasoning and impact.
3. **Memorial.** Write a short written argument for one side of a moot court problem.
4. **Capstone essay.** 2,500 words on one of the Stage 6 questions. Strong essays are read by an expert reviewer.
5. **Explain it.** Answer three unseen questions as if to a friend. For example: “If there's no world police, why do states follow international law?”

The certificate needs every stage passed. It is not a legal qualification.
